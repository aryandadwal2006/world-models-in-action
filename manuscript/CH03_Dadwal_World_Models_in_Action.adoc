= Representation learning before generation

This chapter covers

* The assumptions built into self-supervised objectives
* Contrastive, bootstrap, self-distillation and redundancy-reduction objectives
* Masked prediction and the choice of prediction target
* Latent bottlenecks and the limits of representation evaluation

A world model needs a representation that preserves distinctions relevant to predicting future observations. Pixels contain both useful information and details that a predictive model may not need. An encoder maps the observation latexmath:[x] to a representation latexmath:[z=f_\theta(x)], but compression alone does not make latexmath:[z] useful. Two observations that should lead to different predictions must not be merged merely because their images are similar.

When labels are unavailable, the training signal must be constructed from the data. Methods may identify a matching view among candidates, predict a representation produced by another branch, constrain feature statistics across a batch, or predict content hidden from the encoder. These objectives differ in their targets, their assumptions and their optimization dynamics.

The companion repository keeps commit-pinned links to upstream implementations in its link:https://github.com/aryandadwal2006/world-models-in-action/tree/main-extra/implementations/README.md[official implementation manifest]. Those source pointers preserve provenance; they do not imply that this book independently reproduced the papers' results, and each project has its own dependencies and license.

The central question is therefore not just whether the training loss decreases. It is what the loss rewards the encoder for preserving. A feature may solve the pretraining task yet omit information needed for a later prediction. The following sections derive the principal objectives and explain exactly what their mathematical constraints do and do not establish.

== Self-supervised learning foundations

[sidebar]
.The central distinction
****
Self-supervised learning constructs a relationship from data and optimizes an encoder to satisfy it. That relationship may be useful without being sufficient for a world model. Separate the training target, the learned representation and evidence about downstream state quality.
****

[#unique_section2_ch3_002_representation_not_compression_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== A representation is not just a compressed observation

Let latexmath:[x] be an observation and let latexmath:[f_\theta] be an encoder with parameters latexmath:[\theta]. Its output is a vector latexmath:[z]:

[latexmath]
++++
z=f_\theta(x).
\tag{3.1}
++++

A smaller representation is useful only if the information removed is unnecessary for the task. A vector may preserve object identity while losing location or orientation. It may describe a frame's appearance while losing motion that is apparent only from a sequence. Compression can remove nuisance detail, but it can also merge observations whose futures differ.

Chapter 2 formalized the desired contract in terms of sufficiency, minimality and aliasing. Self-supervised objectives do not observe that contract directly. They rely on a proxy relationship in the data. A representation may perform well on the proxy task while failing to retain information the world model needs.

[#unique_section2_ch3_003_invariance_is_a_decision_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Invariance is a decision

Suppose latexmath:[T_1] and latexmath:[T_2] are random transformations applied to the same input:

[latexmath]
++++
x_1=T_1(x),\qquad x_2=T_2(x).
\tag{3.2}
++++

A view-consistency objective encourages latexmath:[f_\theta(x_1)] and latexmath:[f_\theta(x_2)] to agree. The design decision is hidden in latexmath:[T_1] and latexmath:[T_2]: they define which input differences the model is encouraged to ignore.

Random crops are useful when the cropped-out region is irrelevant to the intended representation. They are harmful when the missing region contains information needed to distinguish the scene or predict its next state. Color jitter can suppress nuisance lighting changes, but it can also remove a visual cue on which a later prediction depends. The transformation is part of the learning problem, not a neutral preprocessing step.

An ideal invariance would identify observations that the downstream task truly treats as equivalent. A practical augmentation only approximates that relation. We should not assume that two views are interchangeable for every task just because they came from the same image.

The distinction can be written directly into the objective. Let latexmath:[T_1,T_2] be random transformations sampled from a chosen distribution and let latexmath:[d] measure disagreement between two features. A consistency objective has the form

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{cons}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,T_1,T_2}
\left[d\!\left(f_\theta(T_1x),f_\theta(T_2x)\right)\right].
++++

The expectation averages over the views the augmentation pipeline actually creates, not every transformation that might occur in the world. If a transformation removes information, the encoder is rewarded for making the two resulting views agree despite that missing information. Information that varies unpredictably across views cannot reliably help reduce this loss. This is how augmentation defines an implicit preference over what the representation keeps.

A useful view changes nuisance factors while leaving the intended shared content recoverable. A crop may preserve object identity but alter visible context; a color transformation may preserve shape but alter appearance. That assumption belongs to the data and task, not to the loss function itself.

This can be made precise. Let latexmath:[\mathcal{T}] be a set of transformations and define the orbit of an input latexmath:[x] as
latexmath:[\mathcal{O}(x)=\{T(x):T\in\mathcal{T}\}].
If an encoder is exactly invariant, then

[latexmath]
++++
f_\theta(Tx)=f_\theta(x)\qquad
\text{for every }T\in\mathcal{T}.
++++

All observations in the same orbit receive the same representation. The encoder therefore acts on equivalence classes of inputs rather than on every pixel-level distinction. This is the mathematical meaning of invariance: it removes distinctions that the chosen transformation family treats as irrelevant.

That reduction is beneficial only if the target of interest is constant within each class. If latexmath:[Y] changes when latexmath:[x] is transformed, no exactly invariant representation can preserve all information about latexmath:[Y]. The problem is not that optimization failed; the objective has asked the encoder to discard the distinction. In real systems the equality is approximate, but the design pressure remains.

Invariance is also different from *equivariance*. An invariant representation remains unchanged under a transformation. An equivariant representation changes in a predictable way. For example, if an image is translated, an image-level category feature may ideally remain fixed, while a spatial feature map may shift in correspondence with the image. Classification and localization therefore need not prefer the same transformation behavior. A feature map that is deliberately equivariant can be more useful than a globally invariant vector when the target depends on where a feature occurs.


Historically, visual self-supervision used hand-designed pretext tasks such as predicting image rotation (Gidaris et al., 2018), restoring the spatial order of jigsaw patches (Noroozi and Favaro, 2016), predicting spatial context (Doersch et al., 2015), and colorizing grayscale images (Zhang et al., 2016). These tasks assign synthetic targets to unlabeled data and make the target-design assumption easy to see. They remain useful conceptual examples, but the architecture survey below focuses on the broader contrastive, bootstrap, statistical, clustering and masked-prediction families that followed.

These earlier tasks are often called pretext tasks: labels are created automatically from the input, and the encoder is trained to solve the constructed task. Their weakness is not that they fail to learn features. It is that the pretext label may be predictable from shortcuts or may require only a subset of the information needed elsewhere. An encoder can predict rotation using scene-layout cues without learning a representation that preserves every detail of the scene. This motivates objectives whose targets arise from relationships between views, statistics of feature batches, or missing content rather than one fixed synthetic label.

[#unique_section2_ch3_005_from_pairs_to_candidate_classification_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== From pairs to candidate classification

A contrastive method begins with an anchor, a related positive example and distractors called negatives. The encoder maps each example to a feature vector. A similarity function scores the anchor against each candidate. Training rewards the positive for receiving a higher score than the negatives.

In the common two-view construction, each source example latexmath:[x_n] is transformed independently twice:

[latexmath]
++++
\tilde x_n^{(1)}=T_n^{(1)}(x_n),\qquad
\tilde x_n^{(2)}=T_n^{(2)}(x_n).
++++

The two views of the same source form a positive pair. Views from other source examples form the negative candidates for that anchor. No class label is required; source identity supplies the pair label. The label therefore means “same source under two transformations,” not necessarily “same semantic class” or “same underlying state.”

If two different source examples are semantically equivalent, treating one as another's negative creates a false negative relative to a semantic objective. This is not an error in cross-entropy. It is a mismatch between the labels implied by pair construction and the relation we ultimately want the representation to encode.

The negative set is part of the statistical problem, not just a source of extra examples. A contrastive loss trains the encoder to preserve distinctions that help identify the positive against the sampled candidates. If negatives are easy, the model can often solve the task using broad visual cues. As training improves, the most probable negatives dominate the gradient and the model is pushed to distinguish harder cases. This can sharpen useful features, but it can also make the learned geometry depend on details of the candidate distribution.

The loss does not tell the model which differences are semantically important. It only supplies a ranking label for the candidates. If the dataset repeatedly pairs two views of the same source image while treating every other image as negative, the representation is encouraged to keep same-source views close and other sampled views relatively separated. Two different images of the same object may still be pushed apart. Methods that use multiple positives, debiased negatives or semantic labels change this constraint, but they also change the learning problem.


For two feature vectors, cosine similarity is their dot product after unit normalization:

[latexmath]
++++
s(z_i,z_j)=\frac{z_i^\top z_j}{\lVert z_i\rVert_2\lVert z_j\rVert_2}.
\tag{3.3}
++++

A temperature latexmath:[\tau>0] scales every similarity before softmax. If two candidates have similar scores, a lower temperature magnifies their difference in the logits and makes the probability distribution more concentrated. It does not simply make every gradient larger: when the positive already has probability close to one, its cross-entropy gradient is close to zero. Temperature changes both the probability assigned to each candidate and the factor relating a logit gradient to the underlying similarity gradient.

Suppose a batch contains latexmath:[N] source images. Two stochastic views of each image give latexmath:[2N] encoded vectors. For anchor latexmath:[i], let latexmath:[p(i)] be the index of its other view. Excluding the anchor itself, every remaining view is a candidate. The NT-Xent loss for that anchor is

[latexmath]
++++
\ell_i=-\log
\frac{\exp(s(z_i,z_{p(i)})/\tau)}
{\sum_{a=1}^{2N}\mathbf{1}[a\ne i]\exp(s(z_i,z_a)/\tau)}.
\tag{3.4}
++++

The batch loss averages this quantity over all latexmath:[2N] anchors:

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{NT\text{-}Xent}}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\ell_i.
++++

In the denominator of each term are one positive and latexmath:[2N-2] negatives. The positive is included in the denominator; the anchor is not. Stating the candidate set explicitly avoids ambiguity because papers do not all use the same symbol for the number of candidates or negatives.

This objective is ordinary softmax cross-entropy. Write latexmath:[a_{ij}=s(z_i,z_j)/\tau], and let latexmath:[q_{ij}] be the softmax probability assigned to candidate latexmath:[j]. Differentiating with respect to each candidate logit gives

[latexmath]
++++
\frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial a_{ij}}
=q_{ij}-\mathbf{1}[j=p(i)].
\tag{3.5}
++++

The positive logit receives gradient latexmath:[q_{i,p(i)}-1], which pushes it upward under gradient descent. A negative logit receives gradient latexmath:[q_{ij}], which pushes it downward. The gradient on a negative is weighted by its current probability: a hard negative receives more repulsive gradient than an already dissimilar candidate. Since latexmath:[a_{ij}=s(z_i,z_j)/\tau], differentiation with respect to the unscaled similarity introduces the factor latexmath:[1/\tau].

For example, suppose one anchor has cosine similarities latexmath:[0.80], latexmath:[0.10] and latexmath:[-0.30] to its positive and two negatives, with latexmath:[\tau=0.5]. The logits are latexmath:[1.6], latexmath:[0.2] and latexmath:[-0.6]. Softmax assigns the positive probability about latexmath:[0.737], so the loss for this anchor is latexmath:[-\log(0.737)\approx0.306]. If all three candidates received equal probability, the loss would be latexmath:[-\log(1/3)\approx1.099]. The loss measures whether the correct candidate is easy to identify relative to the candidates that happened to be sampled.

The logit derivative explains the direction of the update. If the positive has probability latexmath:[q_+], its derivative is latexmath:[q_+-1], which is nonpositive. Gradient descent therefore raises the positive logit unless it is already assigned probability one. A negative with probability latexmath:[q_j] has positive derivative latexmath:[q_j], so its logit is lowered. The gradient focuses on candidates that the model currently finds plausible; easy negatives contribute little because their probabilities are already small.

For a cosine score latexmath:[s(u,v)=\hat u^\top\hat v], where latexmath:[\hat u=u/\lVert u\rVert_2] and latexmath:[\hat v=v/\lVert v\rVert_2], the derivative with respect to the first vector is

[latexmath]
++++
\frac{\partial s(u,v)}{\partial u}
=\frac{1}{\lVert u\rVert_2}
\left(\hat v-s(u,v)\hat u\right).
++++

This gradient changes the direction of a normalized vector to improve its relative score. In a neural network, the same representation can appear as an anchor and as a candidate in other loss terms, so its total gradient accumulates contributions from all those roles. Contrastive learning is not merely “pull positives together”: it adjusts the representation so that positives outrank the negatives in the sampled candidate set.

[#unique_section2_ch3_006_what_infoNCE_does_and_does_not_prove_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== What InfoNCE does and does not prove

InfoNCE is a relative objective. The anchor must assign a higher score to its designated positive than to the sampled alternatives. Maximizing the positive similarity alone would not impose this ranking constraint. With an unnormalized dot product the model can also increase scores by increasing vector norms; cosine normalization bounds the score but does not remove dependence on the candidate-sampling protocol.

The positive label is supplied by data construction. In a two-view image objective, it means that two views came from the same source image. A negative can still depict the same semantic class as the anchor or show a nearly identical state. This is a false negative relative to a semantic objective, not an error in the cross-entropy arithmetic. The transformation distribution creates a related risk: if a view removes a relevant feature, consistency across those views can reward the encoder for ignoring it.

InfoNCE also has an information-theoretic interpretation, but only under a specified sampling construction. Suppose one index latexmath:[i] is selected uniformly from latexmath:[K] candidates; latexmath:[y_i] is sampled from the conditional distribution latexmath:[p(y\mid x)], while the other latexmath:[K-1] candidates are sampled independently from the marginal latexmath:[p(y)]. Bayes' rule gives

[latexmath]
++++
P(i\mid x,y_1,\ldots,y_K)
=\frac{r(x,y_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}r(x,y_j)},
\qquad
r(x,y)=\frac{p(y\mid x)}{p(y)}.
++++

The optimal classifier therefore ranks candidates by the density ratio: a pair receives a high score when it is more likely under the joint distribution than under independent sampling. A learned critic approximates this rule through its logits. The candidate-classification cross-entropy under this sampling construction yields the lower bound

[latexmath]
++++
I(X;Y)\ge \log K-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}},
++++

where latexmath:[K] includes the positive candidate. Equivalently, latexmath:[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}}\ge \log K-I(X;Y)]. The finite candidate count limits this bound to latexmath:[\log K]. The bound does not grow without limit merely because training continues.

The assumptions matter. Nearby frames, repeated examples or multiple instances of one category can violate independent negative sampling. If negatives come from a different distribution, or candidates are dependent, the same bound does not automatically follow. The density-ratio argument explains the role of the loss and its sampling assumptions; it does not turn an arbitrary contrastive loss into a certified measure of semantic information.

Finally, mutual information shared by two views is not the same as sufficiency for a later prediction. A feature can be shared between views yet irrelevant to the future quantity of interest. The information-theoretic bound says something about dependence under the sampling model, not whether all distinctions required by a downstream predictor have been retained.

=== Alignment and uniformity are different geometric requirements

For normalized representations, contrastive learning can be studied through two geometric diagnostics. *Alignment* asks matched views to be close. *Uniformity* asks independent examples not to crowd into a small part of the unit sphere. They explain different pressures in contrastive training, but they are not a universal exact decomposition of every self-supervised loss.

One alignment measure is

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{align}}
=\mathbb E_{(x,x^+)\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}}
\|f(x)-f(x^+)\|_2^\alpha,
++++

where latexmath:[\alpha>0]. A uniformity measure is

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{uniform}}
=\log\mathbb E_{x,y\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}
\exp(-t\|f(x)-f(y)\|_2^2),
++++

where latexmath:[t>0] and x,y are independent samples. Nearby embeddings contribute strongly to the exponential, so crowding raises the quantity. Wang and Isola's (2020) analysis uses normalized features and a specified sampling setup. These measures are a lens on contrastive learning, not permission to describe every SSL algorithm as optimizing the same two losses.

Uniformity is not semantic correctness: features can spread out while encoding background texture or another nuisance variable. Alignment can be excellent because an augmentation erased a task-critical distinction. A useful representation requires invariances to match the prediction problem.

[#unique_section2_ch3_031_energy_based_scores_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== The energy-based view of contrastive scores

An energy-based model assigns a scalar compatibility score, or energy, to a configuration. Lower energy means the model regards the configuration as more compatible with the observed evidence. For an anchor latexmath:[x] and candidate latexmath:[y], let latexmath:[E_\theta(x,y)] be the energy. A conditional distribution can be written

[latexmath]
++++
p_\theta(y\mid x)=
\frac{\exp[-E_\theta(x,y)]}
{\sum_{y'}\exp[-E_\theta(x,y')]}.
++++

The denominator is a partition function: it normalizes the scores over all candidate values latexmath:[y']. If the candidate space is large or continuous, computing that normalizer can be difficult. An unnormalized energy function can still rank alternatives, but ranking alone is not the same as having an explicitly normalized probability model over the full space.

Contrastive learning can be read as energy-based candidate classification. Set latexmath:[E_\theta(x,y)=-s_\theta(x,y)/\tau]. The softmax over the anchor's sampled candidates assigns lower energy to the positive and higher energy to alternatives. The NT-Xent gradient therefore has the same structure as ordinary cross-entropy over candidate energies: lower the positive's energy relative to the negatives, with more gradient assigned to negatives that currently receive high probability. The candidate denominator in InfoNCE is a finite, sampled normalization, not necessarily the full partition function over every possible image or latent.

This connection clarifies both the power and the limit of the method. A learned energy can represent a rich compatibility relation without immediately providing a tractable normalized distribution over all images. If the goal is classification among a fixed candidate set, ranking may be enough. If the goal is generation, likelihood evaluation or drawing samples from the full distribution, additional machinery is needed. Denoising diffusion models, discussed later, use a different training construction to learn a sequence of denoising transitions; they should not be treated as merely a larger InfoNCE denominator.

Energy-based learning is a general framework rather than a synonym for contrastive learning. Some energy-based methods compare observed configurations to generated or sampled alternatives; some train through likelihood or margin-based objectives. InfoNCE is one particular candidate-discrimination loss whose energy interpretation is especially transparent because its normalization and gradient are explicit. See LeCun et al. (2006) for the broader framework.

=== SimCLR and MoCo: two ways to construct candidates

SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020) made the augmented-view recipe explicit. Two views pass through an encoder latexmath:[f] and a nonlinear projection head latexmath:[g], and the objective is applied to latexmath:[z=g(f(x))]. The representation before the head, latexmath:[h=f(x)], is commonly retained for downstream tasks. The projector allows the loss to shape a space specialized for the pretraining comparison, rather than requiring every property of that space to be optimal for later evaluation. This is a design choice supported by the SimCLR experiments, not a theorem that a projection head always improves every representation.

SimCLR's results depended on augmentation composition, batch size, architecture, training duration and optimization. A large batch increases the number of available negatives under an in-batch objective, but changes the classification problem itself and may add more false negatives. Its contribution is a clear recipe for contrastive visual learning, not evidence that every contrastive method needs one specific batch size or projector.

MoCo (He et al., 2020) uses a query encoder and a key encoder, with a queue storing keys from earlier minibatches. In the original recipe, the query and key features are L2-normalized before their dot products are scored. The query feature latexmath:[q=f_{\theta_q}(x_q)] is trained to identify its matching key latexmath:[k^+=f_{\theta_k}(x_k)] among the positive and queued negative keys latexmath:[k_j\in Q]. Its candidate loss has the form

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MoCo}}
=-\log\frac{\exp(q^\top k^+/\tau)}
{\exp(q^\top k^+/\tau)+\sum_{k_j\in Q}\exp(q^\top k_j/\tau)}.
++++

The queue gives each query a larger candidate set than the current minibatch alone. The key branch receives no backpropagation gradient; its parameters follow an exponential moving average of the query encoder:

[latexmath]
++++
\theta_k\leftarrow m\theta_k+(1-m)\theta_q.
\tag{3.6}
++++

After the loss is evaluated against the current queue, keys from the current minibatch are enqueued and the oldest keys are dequeued to keep the queue length fixed. The queue therefore contains features produced by earlier parameter values. They become stale as training proceeds; a slowly moving key encoder reduces abrupt drift in their representation space but cannot make historical keys identical to current ones. This EMA update makes the key parameters a smoothed history of the query parameters: for fixed latexmath:[m], contributions from earlier updates decay geometrically. MoCo v2 (Chen et al., 2020) strengthened v1's augmentation, projection-head and optimization recipe while retaining the queue and momentum key encoder. The MoCo v1/v2 loss above therefore uses positive-plus-queue candidates. MoCo v3 (Chen et al., 2021) removes this persistent FIFO queue. Its reference implementation compares the online prediction for one crop against momentum-encoder targets for the other crop, gathered across workers. With latexmath:[Q_1,Q_2] denoting normalized online predictor outputs, latexmath:[K_1,K_2] the corresponding normalized momentum targets gathered across workers, and latexmath:[y] the matching sample indices, its symmetric batch classification can be written

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{v3}}=
\operatorname{CE}\!\left(\frac{Q_1K_2^\top}{\tau},y\right)
+\operatorname{CE}\!\left(\frac{Q_2K_1^\top}{\tau},y\right).
++++

The momentum encoder remains stop-gradient and EMA-updated; the online path has a predictor. This is not the v1/v2 queue algorithm with a different backbone. The reference code also multiplies each cross-entropy term by a temperature-dependent constant, a loss-scale convention rather than a different candidate relation.

Both methods remain sensitive to the definition of positive pairs and negatives. The queue expands the candidate set, while the momentum encoder mitigates abrupt feature drift without eliminating staleness. Neither mechanism establishes that every negative is semantically different.

[#unique_section2_ch3_034_historical_contrastive_branches_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Historical contrastive branches

SimCLR and MoCo are central modern examples, but their shared candidate-classification structure has earlier branches. These are not simply a sequence of equivalent losses.

**Non-parametric instance discrimination (NPID)** treats each image instance as a proxy class and uses a memory bank of features with a noise-contrastive objective. The signal is instance identity, which need not equal semantic identity.

**PIRL** asks representations to remain compatible across a pretext transformation while preserving an instance-discrimination signal. It changes the positive relation by bridging a transformed view and the original instance. The transformation helps only when the induced invariance matches downstream needs.

**Contrastive Multiview Coding (CMC)** contrasts features from different views or modalities of the same source. The positive relation is a shared source rather than merely two augmentations of the same image tensor; usefulness depends on information shared between views.

**AMDIM** uses local and global representations at multiple feature scales. It contrasts a local feature with a global feature from the same image while mismatched images supply negatives. This introduces spatially indexed dependencies that global instance discrimination may not enforce.

These methods all construct a discrimination problem, but their positive relations and sampling distributions differ. An information-bound interpretation must follow the sampler, not the generic label “contrastive.” See the original papers on [NPID](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2018/html/Wu_Unsupervised_Feature_Learning_CVPR_2018_paper.html), [PIRL](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2020/html/Misra_Self-Supervised_Learning_of_Pretext-Invariant_Representations_CVPR_2020_paper.html), [CMC](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05849) and [AMDIM](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00910).

=== CPC as a historical bridge

Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC; van den Oord et al., 2018) encodes a sequence and uses a context vector from the past to score future latent representations. An InfoNCE-style objective teaches the context to identify the observed future latent among candidate latents. CPC is a useful historical bridge between contrastive discrimination and predictive representations, but it is not the centerpiece of a contemporary visual SSL survey.

A CPC future-latent retrieval score only shows that the correct future code can be identified among the sampled candidates under the chosen objective. It does not by itself establish accurate multi-step rollout, a sufficient predictive state, or that errors remain controlled when predictions are fed back into the model.

== Non-contrastive objectives

Contrastive learning makes a designated positive score higher than sampled negatives. Non-contrastive methods remove that explicit competition, but they still need a mechanism that prevents agreement from becoming trivial. BYOL and SimSiam use asymmetric prediction pathways; DINO matches teacher distributions; Barlow Twins and VICReg constrain batch statistics.

[#unique_section2_ch3_030_agreement_and_collapse_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Why agreement alone admits collapse

Suppose two views of the same input are trained only to produce equal representations. A simple invariance loss is

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{inv}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert z_i-z_i'\rVert_2^2.
++++

If the encoder maps every input to the same constant vector latexmath:[c], then latexmath:[z_i=z_i'=c] for every example and the loss is zero. The objective has no term requiring different inputs to remain distinguishable. A constant output is therefore a collapsed solution, even though it is useless for representing the data.

This observation does not mean every method that matches two views will collapse. It means agreement alone cannot rule collapse out. The architecture, stop-gradient placement, target updates, normalizations and optimization dynamics affect which solutions training reaches. The methods below differ in precisely those mechanisms; none should be described as having a universal guarantee based only on its loss formula.

It is useful to distinguish complete collapse from dimensional collapse. In complete collapse, all inputs map to the same vector and the representation contains no information about differences between examples. In dimensional collapse, the output varies, but most of its covariance is concentrated in a small number of directions. A model can therefore avoid the constant solution and still use its nominal latexmath:[d]-dimensional space inefficiently.

These cases require different diagnostics. Per-coordinate variance detects a constant dimension; the spectrum of the covariance matrix shows whether variation is spread across many directions. But neither diagnostic answers whether those directions contain the information of interest. A feature can vary strongly with background texture and have high effective rank while carrying little information about the target. Anti-collapse is necessary for useful representations, not sufficient.

The covariance spectrum gives a more quantitative description of dimensional collapse. Let latexmath:[\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d] be the nonnegative eigenvalues of the feature covariance matrix. Normalize them into proportions

[latexmath]
++++
p_i=\frac{\lambda_i}{\sum_j\lambda_j},
\qquad
r_{\mathrm{eff}}=\exp\!\left(-\sum_i p_i\log p_i\right).
++++

The quantity latexmath:[r_{\mathrm{eff}}] is an effective-rank diagnostic. If all variance lies in one direction, the distribution latexmath:[p_i] is concentrated and the effective rank is near one. If variance is spread evenly across latexmath:[d] directions, the effective rank is latexmath:[d]. This statistic differs from the count of nonzero dimensions: a representation can have a formally full-rank covariance matrix while almost all its variance lies in a few directions.

Effective rank remains a geometric diagnostic, not a semantic one. A high-rank representation can encode color noise, background texture or image identity; a low-rank representation can still preserve all information required for a simple target. Variance, covariance and effective rank tell us about the distribution of features across a batch. They do not identify what the features mean or prove that a selected future variable is recoverable. That requires a separate target-specific evaluation.

[#unique_section2_ch3_010_byol_and_simsiam_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== BYOL and SimSiam: bootstrap prediction

BYOL (Grill et al., 2020) has an online encoder, a projection head and a predictor, alongside a target encoder and target projection head. Only the online network receives gradients. The target network is updated by an exponential moving average (EMA) of the online encoder and projector; it is not updated by backpropagation.

Let latexmath:[z_1=g_\phi(f_\theta(x_1))], latexmath:[p_1=q_\psi(z_1)], and latexmath:[\bar z_2=g_{\bar\phi}(f_{\bar\theta}(x_2))]. The prediction loss for one direction is the normalized regression

[latexmath]
++++
D(p_1,\mathrm{sg}(\bar z_2))
=2-2\frac{p_1^\top\bar z_2}{\lVert p_1\rVert_2\lVert\bar z_2\rVert_2},
++++

where latexmath:[\mathrm{sg}] denotes stop-gradient. A second term swaps the views. The online predictor is trained to match the target feature, while the target feature is treated as fixed for that gradient calculation.

The target parameters change according to

[latexmath]
++++
\bar\theta\leftarrow m\bar\theta+(1-m)\theta,\qquad
\bar\phi\leftarrow m\bar\phi+(1-m)\phi,
++++

with latexmath:[m] close to one. The target is therefore a smoothed history of the online network, not an independently optimized network. This is the defining difference from ordinary shared-weight Siamese regression.

With fixed momentum latexmath:[m], repeated EMA updates give old parameters geometrically decreasing influence. The target is consequently neither fixed nor an exact copy of the current online network: it is a moving reference that changes more slowly than the optimized branch. This temporal asymmetry is an essential part of BYOL's method, not merely a way to save computation.

For clarity, the two-view objective can be written

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{BYOL}}=
D(p_1,\mathrm{sg}(\bar z_2))
+D(p_2,\mathrm{sg}(\bar z_1)).
++++

During a gradient step, the first term updates the online encoder, projector and predictor through latexmath:[p_1]; it does not update the target parameters through latexmath:[\bar z_2]. The second term reverses the roles of the views. After the gradient update, the EMA rule updates the target parameters. Thus two distinct operations occur: gradient descent optimizes the online network against a fixed target value for that step, and parameter averaging moves the target afterward.

This order explains why an EMA target is more than a detached copy. A detached copy would stop gradients through the target but would not specify how the target changes over training. EMA defines a low-pass update: fast fluctuations in online weights have less immediate effect on the target, while persistent changes gradually pass into it. In the limiting case latexmath:[m=0], the target copies the online weights after each update; as latexmath:[m] approaches one, the target evolves more slowly. This describes the update mechanism, not a universal proof against collapse.



SimSiam (Chen and He, 2021) uses the same encoder and projector for both views and a predictor on each branch, but no EMA target network. With latexmath:[z_i=g(f(x_i))] and latexmath:[p_i=q(z_i)], its symmetric loss has the form

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{SimSiam}}=
D_{\mathrm{SimSiam}}(p_1,\mathrm{sg}(z_2))
+D_{\mathrm{SimSiam}}(p_2,\mathrm{sg}(z_1)),
++++

where latexmath:[D_{\mathrm{SimSiam}}] is negative cosine similarity. The stop-gradient leaves a target's forward value unchanged but removes the gradient path through that target in the corresponding term. Because the loss is symmetric, each shared branch acts as predictor in one term and target in the other. BYOL instead maintains a separate target network whose parameters follow the EMA update.

The predictor and stop-gradient change the optimization dynamics; they do not remove the constant-output solution from the mathematical objective.

The distance used in the symmetric objective can be written

[latexmath]
++++
D_{\mathrm{SimSiam}}(p,z)
=-\frac{p^\top z}{\lVert p\rVert_2\lVert z\rVert_2}.
++++

Minimizing this negative cosine similarity maximizes directional agreement without requiring the two vectors to have the same norm. In latexmath:[D(p_1,\mathrm{sg}(z_2))], the numerical value of latexmath:[z_2] still affects the loss, but its gradient path is removed. The shared encoder can nevertheless receive gradients through latexmath:[z_2] when that representation appears as the predictor-side output in the opposite term. Stop-gradient is applied to a particular *role* of the representation in each term, not as a permanent property of the tensor.

This distinction matters when interpreting a computational graph. “The target branch has no gradient” is accurate for a single detached target path; it would be inaccurate to say that the shared encoder receives no gradient from the second view across the full symmetric loss. The symmetry of the loss and the asymmetry of each gradient path coexist.

 The original SimSiam study found that stop-gradient was essential to its proposed configuration. That is a method-specific result, not a theorem that any Siamese network with a detached branch must learn non-collapsed features.

The predictor makes the two sides of the training relation structurally asymmetric. The online path must learn a transformation latexmath:[q] whose output matches the other branch's representation, while the target path's value is held fixed for that loss term. If the target also received gradients from the same term, the two sides could reduce the discrepancy by moving together immediately. Stop-gradient prevents that direct route for the target side, but it does not by itself force informative features. The predictor, normalization, shared parameters and optimization dynamics together determine whether training moves away from collapse in the original recipes.

[#unique_section2_ch3_011_dino_and_self_distillation_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== DINO: match a teacher's distribution

DINO (Caron et al., 2021) matches distributions rather than regressing one feature vector onto another. The student and teacher map different views to logits over learned prototype outputs. If latexmath:[a_s(x)] and latexmath:[a_t(x)] are their logits, the student and teacher distributions are

[latexmath]
++++
p_s(x)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{a_s(x)}{T_s}\right),\qquad
q_t(x)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{a_t(x)-c}{T_t}\right).
++++

Here latexmath:[c] is a running center of teacher outputs, latexmath:[T_t] controls teacher-target sharpness, and the teacher parameters follow an EMA of the student parameters. The student is trained across selected, different teacher-student views. For a target distribution latexmath:[q_t], the student's cross-entropy is

[latexmath]
++++
H(q_t,p_s)=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{t,k}\log p_{s,k}.
++++

Training sums this loss over selected teacher-student view pairs. Centering subtracts a running output mean before the teacher softmax; sharpening uses a low teacher temperature to lower target entropy. They serve different roles: centering reduces persistent preference for a subset of output coordinates, while sharpening produces a more decisive target distribution.

Cross-entropy decomposes into the entropy of the target plus a divergence:

[latexmath]
++++
H(q_t,p_s)=H(q_t)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_t\Vert p_s).
++++

For a fixed teacher target, the entropy term is constant with respect to the student, so minimizing cross-entropy makes the student distribution approach the teacher distribution. The decomposition also clarifies a collapse risk. If the teacher produces the same distribution for every input, the student can match it with a small KL divergence while learning little input-dependent structure. A low loss by itself does not show that the teacher's targets vary meaningfully across examples.

Centering and sharpening address different undesirable target distributions. Without centering, one output coordinate can dominate across many inputs, encouraging the teacher to repeatedly favor the same prototype. Centering counteracts this dominance, but by itself may encourage overly uniform outputs. Sharpening lowers the teacher temperature, making each target distribution more concentrated rather than uniform. Their combination helps retain input-dependent assignments. These are practical anti-collapse mechanisms of the DINO recipe; they should not be generalized into a theorem that centering and sharpening alone guarantee useful features in every architecture or training setup.


Prototype indices are learned output coordinates, not automatically human-defined categories. DINO is best understood as self-distillation with a prototype distribution, rather than as an ordinary clustering algorithm.

Multi-crop training supplies a useful extra constraint. The teacher receives the larger global crops, while the student also sees smaller local crops. A local crop usually lacks some of the global context, so its output must match a target produced from a different field of view. This encourages some features to remain consistent across scales. It does not mean that every local crop contains enough evidence to infer every property of the entire scene; the target and crop protocol define what consistency is encouraged. The prototype outputs are learned coordinates, not labels assigned by a person, and their semantics must be established through evaluation.

These releases also illustrate a general caution: reported improvements belong to a complete training system, including data, architecture, optimization and evaluation, rather than to an isolated loss substitution. The following details separate the original DINO mechanism from components added in later recipes.


DINOv2 is a combined training recipe, not simply a replacement loss: it combines class-token DINO distillation, iBOT-style masked patch distillation and a KoLeo nearest-neighbour regularizer. The class branch matches teacher distributions across crops; the patch branch predicts teacher prototype distributions at masked student positions; KoLeo discourages local crowding in normalized backbone features. Depending on configuration, targets may use centering or Sinkhorn-Knopp balancing. Centering subtracts a running mean of teacher logits; Sinkhorn-Knopp iteratively balances assignment marginals. They are not synonyms.

DINOv3 retains related distillation machinery and adds large-scale data, architecture and training choices. Gram anchoring, when enabled, matches teacher and student pairwise patch-similarity matrices rather than requiring coordinate-by-coordinate equality of feature vectors. It is a relational constraint, not proof of physical-state sufficiency, and it is configuration-dependent. See [DINOv2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07193), [DINOv3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10104) and their source configurations.

[#unique_section2_ch3_012_redundancy_reduction_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Redundancy reduction and explicit variance constraints

BYOL, SimSiam and DINO make training dynamics or teacher targets central to avoiding trivial solutions. Barlow Twins (Zbontar et al., 2021) and VICReg (Bardes et al., 2022) express another idea: require paired views to agree, but constrain the statistics of features across a batch.


[#unique_section2_ch3_013_barlow_twins_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Barlow Twins: cross-correlation toward identity

Let latexmath:[Z^A] and latexmath:[Z^B] be feature batches from two views. For dimension latexmath:[i], let latexmath:[\mu_i^A] and latexmath:[\sigma_i^A] be its mean and standard deviation across the batch, and likewise for view latexmath:[B]. Standardize each dimension:

[latexmath]
++++
\tilde z^A_{b,i}=\frac{z^A_{b,i}-\mu_i^A}{\sigma_i^A+\epsilon},
\qquad
\tilde z^B_{b,i}=\frac{z^B_{b,i}-\mu_i^B}{\sigma_i^B+\epsilon}.
++++

Then define the cross-correlation matrix latexmath:[C]:

[latexmath]
++++
C_{ij}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\tilde z^A_{b,i}\tilde z^B_{b,j}.
\tag{3.7}
++++

If corresponding dimensions agree and distinct dimensions are uncorrelated across views, latexmath:[C] approaches the identity matrix. Barlow Twins minimizes the diagonal mismatch and penalizes off-diagonal cross-correlation:

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{BT}}=\sum_i(1-C_{ii})^2+
\lambda\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}C_{ij}^{2}.
\tag{3.8}
++++

If latexmath:[C_{ii}] is below one, the corresponding coordinate is not perfectly aligned across views. A nonzero latexmath:[C_{ij}] for latexmath:[i\ne j] means coordinate latexmath:[i] in one view is linearly correlated with coordinate latexmath:[j] in the other. The loss pushes the cross-correlation matrix toward identity. It controls second-order cross-view statistics; it does not establish statistical independence for arbitrary distributions, nor does it assign a meaningful physical interpretation to each coordinate.

The diagonal and off-diagonal penalties have different purposes. The diagonal term encourages coordinate latexmath:[i] from view A to align with coordinate latexmath:[i] from view B. The off-diagonal term discourages coordinate latexmath:[i] in one view from correlating with coordinate latexmath:[j\ne i] in the other. Barlow Twins does not merely minimize the distance between the two feature vectors: it specifies a pattern for the entire batch cross-correlation matrix.

For intuition, suppose two projected coordinates are duplicates, latexmath:[z_2=z_1]. Their off-diagonal cross-correlation is high, so Barlow Twins penalizes that redundancy. If a coordinate does not agree across views, its diagonal correlation falls below one and incurs a diagonal penalty. The objective therefore combines cross-view agreement with pressure against repeated linear information across dimensions. Because the features are standardized over the batch, the correlation matrix describes patterns across examples, not the similarity of two coordinates within a single example.

The standardization is important: the loss is not simply comparing raw feature magnitudes. It measures how two coordinates co-vary across the batch after centering and scaling. A diagonal entry close to one means that a coordinate in the first view tracks its counterpart in the second view; an off-diagonal entry close to zero means that two different coordinates do not exhibit linear cross-view correlation under this batch distribution.

The coefficient latexmath:[\lambda] controls the penalty for off-diagonal correlation relative to diagonal mismatch. If it is small, the objective prioritizes cross-view agreement and permits more redundancy. If it is large, decorrelation receives more weight, but decorrelation is still only a second-order constraint: two coordinates can be uncorrelated while sharing nonlinear dependence. The method reduces one measurable form of redundancy; it does not make each coordinate independent in the full probabilistic sense.

[#unique_section2_ch3_014_vicreg_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== VICReg: make three constraints explicit

VICReg separates invariance, variance and covariance. For two feature batches latexmath:[Z] and latexmath:[Z'], the invariance term is a mean-squared difference:

[latexmath]
++++
s(Z,Z')=\frac{1}{N}\lVert Z-Z'\rVert_F^2.
\tag{3.9}
++++

For each feature dimension latexmath:[j], let latexmath:[\sigma_j(Z)] be its standard deviation over the batch. A variance penalty puts a floor under each dimension's standard deviation:

[latexmath]
++++
v(Z)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}
\max\!\left(0,\gamma-\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Z_j)+\epsilon}\right).
\tag{3.10}
++++

The covariance term penalizes the squared off-diagonal entries of the empirical covariance matrix:

[latexmath]
++++
c(Z)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i\ne j}\operatorname{Cov}(Z)_{ij}^{2}.
\tag{3.11}
++++

The complete objective combines the three terms:

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{VICReg}}=\lambda s(Z,Z')
+\mu\,[v(Z)+v(Z')]
+\nu\,[c(Z)+c(Z')],
++++

where latexmath:[\lambda,\mu,\nu] set their relative weights. The invariance term aligns paired views. The variance term penalizes a feature dimension whose standard deviation falls below latexmath:[\gamma]. The covariance term discourages linear redundancy between different dimensions. The original VICReg variance term uses an unsquared hinge. Once a feature's standard deviation reaches the margin, that dimension contributes zero variance penalty; the objective enforces a floor rather than rewarding ever-increasing variance.

The sample covariance used for latexmath:[c(Z)] is computed after centering the feature columns. For a batch latexmath:[Z\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}], let latexmath:[\mu_j=N^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{N}z_{b,j}]. Its covariance entries are

[latexmath]
++++
\operatorname{Cov}(Z)_{ij}
=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{b=1}^{N}
(z_{b,i}-\mu_i)(z_{b,j}-\mu_j).
++++

The variance penalty and covariance penalty are deliberately different. A coordinate can have sufficient variance while duplicating another coordinate; covariance regularization penalizes that redundancy. A coordinate can have low covariance with every other coordinate yet be nearly constant; the variance penalty addresses that case. The invariance term then asks the two views to agree. These terms are complementary, not interchangeable.

The reductions are part of the definition. In latexmath:[s(Z,Z')], the squared feature-vector difference is summed over coordinates and averaged over examples; it is not automatically the same scalar as elementwise mean-squared error that divides by both batch size and feature dimension. That difference rescales the invariance term by the embedding dimension, changing the effective balance between latexmath:[\lambda], latexmath:[\mu] and latexmath:[\nu]. When comparing equations and implementations, check whether the norm is summed per example or averaged per coordinate before interpreting the coefficients.

A fully constant representation makes latexmath:[s(Z,Z')=0] and makes all off-diagonal covariances zero, but it also makes each standard deviation zero. For any positive margin latexmath:[\gamma], the variance term is therefore positive. In this sense, the variance floor makes exact constant collapse incompatible with minimizing all three terms simultaneously. This explicit penalty differs from BYOL or SimSiam, where a constant representation can make the view-agreement part of the objective small. It still does not guarantee that optimization never reaches a bad solution or that nonconstant directions encode the desired semantics.



The methods are related but not identical. Barlow Twins uses normalized cross-correlation as the central matrix objective. VICReg uses a direct invariance term, a standard-deviation floor and a covariance penalty. Their shared lesson is that view agreement and non-collapse are separate requirements. Their moment constraints are not a guarantee of a world-model-ready state.

A simple two-coordinate example makes the distinction concrete. If both coordinates are copies of the same varying scalar, their variances may exceed the margin, so the variance term is satisfied; however, their covariance is high and the covariance term penalizes them. If the second coordinate is constant, its covariance with the first may be zero, but its standard deviation is below the margin and the variance term penalizes it. Only a feature batch with adequate spread and sufficiently low linear redundancy can make both statistical penalties small. The remaining variation can still describe the wrong thing, so a separate task-based evaluation is necessary.

The synthesis schematic compares these mechanisms without implying that they were trained under a common recipe or that the diagram ranks them.

[#ch3-listing-vicreg,reftext={chapter}.{counter:listing},caption='{listing-caption}.{counter:listing-number} ']
.Three VICReg terms in the official PyTorch implementation (source excerpt)
[source,python]
----
# From facebookresearch/vicreg/main_vicreg.py; both views are projected above.
repr_loss = F.mse_loss(x, y)

x = x - x.mean(dim=0)
y = y - y.mean(dim=0)
std_x = torch.sqrt(x.var(dim=0) + 0.0001)
std_y = torch.sqrt(y.var(dim=0) + 0.0001)
std_loss = torch.mean(F.relu(1 - std_x)) / 2 + torch.mean(F.relu(1 - std_y)) / 2

cov_x = (x.T @ x) / (self.args.batch_size - 1)
cov_y = (y.T @ y) / (self.args.batch_size - 1)
cov_loss = off_diagonal(cov_x).pow_(2).sum().div(self.num_features) + \
           off_diagonal(cov_y).pow_(2).sum().div(self.num_features)

loss = (self.args.sim_coeff * repr_loss
        + self.args.std_coeff * std_loss
        + self.args.cov_coeff * cov_loss)
----

[#unique_section2_ch3_033_distribution_shape_regularization_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Beyond covariance: SIGReg, LeJEPA and VISReg

VICReg constrains variance and off-diagonal covariance. These are interpretable second-order statistics, but two distributions can share a mean and covariance while differing in multimodality or tail behavior. SIGReg and VISReg investigate distribution-shape constraints, particularly for joint-embedding predictive training.

LeJEPA's theory argues that an isotropic Gaussian embedding distribution is optimal for downstream prediction risk under the assumptions developed in its paper. For a standard Gaussian latexmath:[Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)], every unit-direction projection latexmath:[w^\top Z] is standard normal. This is a theory result under specified conditions, not a universal claim that every downstream task requires Gaussian latents.

**SIGReg** approximates distribution matching with random one-dimensional projections. For projected samples latexmath:[u_1,\ldots,u_B], the empirical characteristic function is

[latexmath]
++++
\widehat\varphi_B(t)=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}e^{itu_b},
\qquad
\varphi_{\mathcal N(0,1)}(t)=e^{-t^2/2}.
++++

For random unit directions latexmath:[w_1,\ldots,w_K], a schematic form of this discrepancy is

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{SIGReg}}(Z)
=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\mathbb R}
\left|
\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}e^{itw_k^\top z_b}
-e^{-t^2/2}
\right|^2\omega(t)\,dt,
++++

where latexmath:[\omega(t)] is the weighting used by the prescribed Epps-Pulley-style test. The practical implementation approximates the integral with its specified quadrature points and weights. The Cramér-Wold theorem motivates the sketching argument: all one-dimensional projections together characterize a distribution. A finite collection of projections is only an approximation, not proof of exact multivariate Gaussianity. Quadrature, weighting and normalization must follow the source.

**LeJEPA** combines a predictive objective with SIGReg. It constructs a target from the mean embeddings of global views of the same source instance, then predicts that target from other views. Views must remain grouped by original image; averaging across images defines a different objective. A schematic objective is

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{LeJEPA}}
=(1-\lambda)\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pred}}
+\lambda\mathcal L_{\mathrm{SIGReg}}.
++++

The paper's recipe is designed not to require an EMA target encoder and stop-gradient target branch in the same way as BYOL/SimSiam. That is a different design, not evidence that earlier asymmetries are unnecessary for every task. The full JEPA predictor architecture is reserved for Chapter 4.

**VISReg** separates scale, shape and center. Keep two distinct meanings of *center* separate: the prediction target is the mean embedding of global views of the same source image; the regularizer's center term penalizes the minibatch mean for each view.

In the source-inspected [reference implementation](https://github.com/galilai-group/stable-pretraining/blob/ab836bf699a2be712dfcf980a5eb70d36391e876/stable_pretraining/methods/visreg.py), the projected embeddings have shape \(Z\in\mathbb R^{V\times B\times d}\), with \(V\) views, minibatch size \(B\) and feature dimension \(d\). For each view \(v\), the batch mean and centered embeddings are

[latexmath]
++++
\mu_{v,j}=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}z_{v,b,j},
\qquad
\widehat z_{v,b,j}=z_{v,b,j}-\mu_{v,j}.
++++

The implementation's coordinate scale is the population standard deviation with a small numerical offset:

[latexmath]
++++
\sigma_{v,j}=
\sqrt{\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}\widehat z_{v,b,j}^{\,2}}+\epsilon.
++++

Its scale term pulls those standard deviations toward one:

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{scale}}=
\frac1{Vd}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\sum_{j=1}^{d}
(\sigma_{v,j}-1)^2.
++++

For the shape term, the centered features are normalized by a *detached* copy of the scale vector. The stop-gradient is applied to the denominator, and no second epsilon is added after that scale has already been stabilized:

[latexmath]
++++
\widetilde z_{v,b,j}
=\frac{\widehat z_{v,b,j}}{\operatorname{sg}(\sigma_{v,j})}.
++++

For each randomly sampled unit direction \(w_k\), project the normalized features and sort the \(B\) values. Let \(q_b=\Phi^{-1}(b/(B+1))\) be the corresponding standard-normal quantiles for \(b=1,\ldots,B\). The reference implementation averages the squared differences over views, projection directions and batch positions:

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{shape}}=
\frac1{VKB}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\left\|\operatorname{sort}(\widetilde Z_v w_k)-q_{1:B}\right\|_2^2.
++++

The batch-center penalty is

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{center}}=
\frac1{Vd}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\|\mu_v\|_2^2.
++++

The complete regularizer keeps three explicit weights:

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{Reg}}=
\lambda_{\mathrm{scale}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{scale}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{shape}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{shape}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{center}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{center}}.
++++

The full reference recipe also has a separate *view-invariance* term. For each source image \(b\), let \(c_b\) be the mean of its global-view projections. The model matches every global and local projection \(z_{v,b}\) to that same-image center:

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{inv}}=
\frac1{VBd}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\|z_{v,b}-c_b\|_2^2,
\qquad
c_b=\frac1{V_g}\sum_{v\in\mathcal V_g}z_{v,b}.
++++

The training loss mixes this invariance term with the regularizer,
\(\mathcal L=(1-\lambda_{\mathrm{mix}})\mathcal L_{\mathrm{inv}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{mix}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{Reg}}\).
Thus the *same-image global-view center* is the target for view consistency, whereas the *per-view minibatch mean* is penalized by \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{center}}\). They are different operations. SIGReg instead uses a characteristic-function discrepancy; VISReg compares sorted projected samples with Gaussian quantiles. The stop-gradient here decouples scale and shape gradients; it is not a BYOL-style teacher pathway.

The VISReg authors report stronger correction near collapse and favorable results on tested out-of-distribution, low-rank and long-tailed settings. These are reported findings, not guarantees for every optimizer or data distribution. Report batch size, projection count, objective weights, architecture and evaluation protocol when comparing these methods. See [LeJEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08544) and [VISReg](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02572).

=== Other methods in brief

**DeepCluster** alternates between clustering the current feature vectors and training the encoder to predict the resulting cluster assignments. The pseudo-labels therefore change as the representation changes. **SwAV** computes prototype assignments online and predicts one view's assignment from another view's features; a balancing procedure discourages all examples from being assigned to a small number of prototypes. Both methods turn unlabeled features into pseudo-targets, but differ in how assignments are generated and refreshed.



The table is an index, not a claim that these systems share one objective. In image-text training, **CLIP** uses normalized image and text embeddings, a learned logit scale and cross-entropy in both image-to-text and text-to-image directions. **SigLIP** uses pairwise sigmoid/logistic terms for matched and mismatched pairs rather than the same batchwise softmax. Natural-language descriptions are supervision from data even though they are not manually assigned class labels.

For speech, **wav2vec 2.0** masks latent speech frames and contrasts context features against quantized targets; **HuBERT** predicts cluster-derived targets at masked positions; **data2vec** predicts contextualized teacher features. For language, BERT predicts selected corrupted tokens from bidirectional context, GPT-style causal language models predict the next token from a prefix, and ELECTRA discriminates observed tokens from generator-replaced tokens. Context and target distributions differ. Generative modeling returns in later chapters: diffusion and energy-based methods have different target and probability/sampling contracts, so they should not be folded into the same family merely because the target is data-derived.

[#unique_section2_ch3_022_predictive_latent_targets_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Predicting target representations

Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) predict a representation of a target region or future segment from a representation of its context. They need not reconstruct target pixels or identify the target among explicitly sampled negatives.

Let latexmath:[x] be an image or video clip, with context positions latexmath:[C] and target positions latexmath:[Y]. A context encoder sees only the context input,

[latexmath]
++++
z_C=f_\theta(x_C,C),
++++

while a target encoder produces features from the original or less-corrupted observation,

[latexmath]
++++
h_Y=f_{\bar\theta}(x)_Y.
++++

The predictor uses context embeddings and target-position information,

[latexmath]
++++
\widehat h_Y=g_\phi(z_C,C,Y),
++++

to minimize a target-feature error such as

[latexmath]
++++
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pred}}
=\frac{1}{|Y|d}\sum_{r\in Y}
\|\widehat h_r-\mathrm{sg}(h_r)\|_2^2.
++++

Here sg denotes the stop-gradient operator. Particular papers use different norms, normalization, layer targets, reductions and mask protocols; these are method-defining details.

The **context encoder** sees only visible/context tokens, so it cannot copy target tokens from that path. The **target encoder** supplies target embeddings and commonly receives no loss gradients; standard I-JEPA/V-JEPA recipes update it by an exponential moving average of the online encoder. The **predictor** consumes context features and target locations to infer selected target embeddings. It is not a pixel decoder.

**I-JEPA** uses context regions and multiple target blocks in an image. **V-JEPA** extends the target relation across space and time in video clips. The masking protocol determines which locations are hidden and what information is available to the predictor.

**V-JEPA 2** separates action-free video pretraining from **V-JEPA 2-AC**, an action-conditioned post-training stage using interaction trajectories. Passive video can teach correlations in observed motion, but a predictor with no action input is not directly trained to answer which future follows a chosen action. Conditioning on actions changes the prediction problem; correctness still depends on state-action coverage and testing under interventions.

**V-JEPA 2.1** adds dense predictive loss, intermediate-layer self-supervision and multimodal image/video tokenizers to the published recipe. Dense loss can include context as well as masked tokens, and deep self-supervision changes which internal features receive direct training signals. These are recipe-level modifications; results should not be attributed to an isolated term without controlled ablations.

The boundary with CPC is precise. CPC scores a future latent against sampled candidate latents using contrastive identification. JEPA predicts target embeddings at selected positions without requiring an explicit negative-candidate classification objective in its core loss. The boundary with MAE is equally important: MAE predicts target pixels; JEPA predicts target features. Neither relation alone proves that the learned representation is a sufficient state for every downstream world model.

Chapter 4 develops JEPA architecture and predictive representations in detail. Here the key lesson is that target choice changes what information the loss rewards. Predicting in latent space avoids a pixel-reconstruction requirement, but the representation can still omit task-critical information.

== Masked prediction: choose what the model must recover

[#unique_section2_ch3_017_one_mask_many_targets_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== One mask, several target spaces

Suppose an input is divided into visible positions latexmath:[V] and masked positions latexmath:[M]. An encoder processes the allowed context and a prediction head estimates a target latexmath:[y_i] at masked positions:

[latexmath]
++++
L=\frac{1}{|M|}\sum_{i\in M}\ell(\hat y_i,y_i).
\tag{3.12}
++++

This form leaves the crucial choice open: what is the target? It might be pixel values, a discrete code, a word token, a replaced-token label or a contextualized feature. The mask tells the model where information is withheld; the target tells it what relationship must be learned.

For a probabilistic predictor, the general objective can be written as a conditional negative log-likelihood:

[latexmath]
++++
L(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{X_V,Y_M}
\left[\log p_\theta(Y_M\mid X_V)\right],
++++

where latexmath:[X_V] is the visible context and latexmath:[Y_M] is the hidden target. If latexmath:[Y_M] is a discrete token or code, this becomes cross-entropy. If the model uses a Gaussian likelihood with fixed variance for a continuous target, minimizing negative log-likelihood is equivalent, up to constants and scale, to minimizing squared error. Under squared error, the optimal predictor in expectation is the conditional mean latexmath:[\mathbb{E}(Y_M\mid X_V)]. Under cross-entropy, the optimal predicted class distribution is the conditional distribution of the target given the visible context.

The distinction appears directly in the loss. With squared-error regression, a predictor is rewarded for numerical proximity to a target vector. With cross-entropy, it is rewarded for assigning probability to the correct discrete label or teacher distribution. These losses define different geometries of error. For a discrete token, predicting a related code is still wrong unless the correct code receives probability; for a continuous target, errors are penalized according to their magnitude in the chosen feature space.

Masking also changes what the model is allowed to observe. A model trained to reconstruct a hidden patch from nearby visible pixels can exploit local texture continuity. A model trained to predict a contextualized teacher feature must reproduce a target shaped by the teacher's wider context and representation space. Neither target is neutral: it specifies which aspects of the missing content distinguish a good prediction from a bad one.

The target-space schematic holds the mask fixed and changes only the prediction target.

[#unique_section2_ch3_018_mae_pixels_and_asymmetry_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== MAE: predict masked pixels

A masked autoencoder (MAE; He et al., 2022) divides an image into patches and hides a large fraction of them. Only visible patches pass through the encoder. A smaller decoder receives the encoded visible patches and learned mask tokens inserted at the missing positions; it predicts pixel values at the original patch locations. The asymmetric design saves encoder computation because the encoder never processes the hidden tokens.

Let latexmath:[m_i=1] when patch latexmath:[i] is masked and latexmath:[0] otherwise, with patch target latexmath:[x_i] and prediction latexmath:[\hat x_i]. The reconstruction objective is

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{MAE}}=
\frac{\sum_i m_i\,\lVert x_i-\hat x_i\rVert_2^2}
{\sum_i m_i}.
++++

The normalization is over masked patches, so visible patches do not contribute to the loss. Under squared error, the conditional mean is the best prediction in expected squared-error terms. If several patch contents are plausible from the visible context, the conditional mean can blur those alternatives. Pixel reconstruction can nevertheless learn useful features; its limitation is that pixel accuracy does not guarantee that the retained encoder feature preserves every variable needed for a later prediction. The encoder, rather than the reconstruction decoder, is usually the component transferred to a downstream task.

The original MAE recipe uses a high masking ratio, 75% of image patches. The encoder therefore processes a sparse visible subset, reducing token-processing cost. The mask ratio also controls prediction difficulty: with few masked patches, more of the image is directly available as context; with too many, the visible content may not determine the missing patches well. The 75% setting is characteristic of the original recipe, not a universal optimum for every resolution, patch size, decoder or target.

[#unique_section2_ch3_019_beit_ibot_and_data2vec_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== BEiT, iBOT and data2vec: change the target

BEiT (Bao et al., 2022) predicts discrete visual-token IDs produced by a learned tokenizer. Rather than regress pixel values, the model classifies the tokenizer's code for each masked patch. The tokenizer introduces its own prior: useful structure can be learned from the code vocabulary, but tokenizer errors and biases enter the target.

iBOT (Zhou et al., 2022) combines masked patch prediction with teacher-student self-distillation. SimMIM (Xie et al., 2022) uses pixel reconstruction; MaskFeat (Wei et al., 2022) predicts visual features such as HOG; BEiT v2 (Peng et al., 2022) improves the discrete-token route with a semantic visual tokenizer. These methods change the target while keeping the general idea of withholding input content. The target families can be written schematically as

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{BEiT}}=-\log p_\theta(k_i\mid x_{\mathrm{visible}}),
\qquad
L_{\mathrm{iBOT}}=-\sum_k q_{t,i,k}\log p_{s,i,k},
++++

where latexmath:[k_i] is the tokenizer's discrete code for a masked patch and latexmath:[q_{t,i}] is a teacher distribution over prototype outputs for patch latexmath:[i]. BEiT assigns probability to a discrete target; iBOT matches a teacher distribution. For data2vec, a contextualized teacher vector latexmath:[y_i^t] supplies a continuous target and the student predicts that vector under a regression loss such as

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{data2vec},i}=\lVert \hat y_i-y_i^t\rVert_2^2.
++++

These schematic expressions show the target-space distinction; each paper has further implementation choices, including the teacher, normalization, masking protocol and the subset of positions used in its loss. They are not full reproductions of those objectives. The differences reinforce the central point: the mask alone does not define the learning task.

These objectives predict a target *conditioned on what remains visible*. They do not, by themselves, specify a full generative model of the input distribution. A masked predictor may estimate a conditional mean, a discrete target distribution or a teacher feature. It need not define a normalized joint distribution over every possible image or support sampling a complete image. A generative model has additional requirements: its factorization or sampling process must define how a complete observation can be generated. This distinction prevents “reconstructs a patch” from being confused with “learns a generative model of images.”

The target also controls the granularity of the error signal. Pixel regression distinguishes exact pixel values and penalizes large numerical deviations more than small ones. Discrete-code cross-entropy cares whether probability is assigned to the correct code, with similarities between codes not automatically reflected in the loss. Teacher-feature regression uses distances in a learned embedding space, so two different target patches may be considered close if the teacher represents them similarly. No target is universally best: the choice decides what counts as a meaningful prediction error.


data2vec (Baevski et al., 2022) predicts contextualized teacher features from masked input and was designed as a framework spanning speech, vision and language. A teacher feature has already aggregated context and training biases from the teacher; it is not simply the original pixel content transformed by a fixed operation. This can make the target more structured, but the student also inherits omissions and biases in the teacher's feature space.

BERT-style masked language modeling predicts selected corrupted tokens from bidirectional context. GPT-style causal language modeling predicts the next token from preceding tokens only. ELECTRA trains a discriminator to detect replaced tokens. These are brief analogies for target/context construction, not additional implementation tutorials.

[#unique_section2_ch3_032_diffusion_and_masked_prediction_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Masked prediction and diffusion solve different problems

Masked prediction and diffusion both train a network to infer a target from a corrupted input, but their corruption processes and objectives differ. A masked autoencoder hides a subset of patch positions and predicts the content at those positions. The visible context remains uncorrupted, and the loss is usually restricted to the masked positions. Diffusion instead corrupts the data by adding noise at a sampled noise level. In the standard denoising diffusion formulation, a clean sample latexmath:[x_0] and Gaussian noise latexmath:[\epsilon] produce

[latexmath]
++++
x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+
\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,
\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).
++++

A network conditioned on latexmath:[x_t] and the noise level latexmath:[t] can be trained to predict the added noise:

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{diff}}=
\mathbb{E}_{x_0,t,\epsilon}
\left[\lVert\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\rVert_2^2\right].
++++

This is a schematic form of the common noise-prediction objective; diffusion papers also use other parameterizations and weighting schemes. Unlike the masked-patch loss, the target is the corruption noise or an equivalent score-related quantity rather than the missing clean pixels at a fixed set of positions.

The distinction is functional. A masked encoder is commonly pretrained to produce representations that can be transferred after its reconstruction decoder is removed. A diffusion model is trained as part of a reverse denoising process that can transform noise into samples from a learned data distribution. The learned denoiser may contain useful representations, but a diffusion loss by itself does not define a compact predictive state or guarantee that its internal activations are suitable for a downstream prediction task. Conversely, a masked-reconstruction encoder is not automatically a generative model just because its decoder predicts missing pixels.

The corruption level is also used differently. MAE usually samples a mask and predicts its hidden positions; the network need not learn a continuous schedule of noise levels or a sequence of reverse transitions. Diffusion samples noise levels from a schedule and learns a family of denoising functions, one for each noise level. Sampling then applies a series of reverse steps, rather than reconstructing a single masked image in one decoder pass. The objective is connected to denoising score matching and, under the diffusion model construction, to a generative likelihood bound. That generative interpretation is distinct from the representation-learning claim made by a successful masked-prediction encoder.

The practical boundary for this chapter is narrow: MAE, BEiT and iBOT illustrate ways to learn from hidden content by selecting different targets; diffusion illustrates how a denoising objective can instead be organized into a generative model. The mathematical resemblance of predicting a corruption target does not make the resulting model or learned feature space equivalent.

[#unique_section2_ch3_024_bottleneck_dimension_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Dimension is capacity, not useful information

A latent bottleneck maps a high-dimensional observation or token grid into a constrained representation. The simplest form is a vector latexmath:[h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}]. Lowering latexmath:[d] limits the number of coordinates, but dimension alone does not measure useful information. A small vector may preserve the few variables needed for a prediction; a wider vector may devote many dimensions to texture, illumination or other nuisance variation.

The information-bottleneck principle expresses this trade-off using mutual information. Let latexmath:[X] be the observation, latexmath:[Z] its representation and latexmath:[Y] a target relevant to the later task. A conceptual objective is

[latexmath]
++++
\min \; I(X;Z)-\beta I(Z;Y).
++++

The first term penalizes information about the input retained by latexmath:[Z]; the second rewards information about latexmath:[Y]. The coefficient latexmath:[\beta] controls the trade-off. This is a theoretical lens, not the loss explicitly optimized by every method in this chapter. Mutual information for deterministic continuous representations requires care; practical SSL objectives usually optimize a tractable proxy.

The information bottleneck gives a useful idealization of *minimal sufficiency*. If latexmath:[Z] is a deterministic function of latexmath:[X] and is sufficient for latexmath:[Y], then latexmath:[Y\perp X\mid Z], so

[latexmath]
++++
I(Z;Y)=I(X;Y).
++++

The representation has retained all information that latexmath:[X] provides about latexmath:[Y], even if it has discarded many details of latexmath:[X] unrelated to latexmath:[Y]. A minimal sufficient representation seeks to retain that predictive information while minimizing what it carries about the rest of the input. The information-bottleneck objective expresses a trade-off, not a direct recipe for finding a unique vector. Different representations can be equally sufficient, especially when features are transformed by invertible maps.



The important question is not “how small can the vector be?” It is “which distinctions can be discarded without changing the predictions we need to make?” A representation useful for image retrieval may be insufficient for predicting motion if it merges observations with different velocities or hidden states.

This is why raw dimension is not the same as information rate. A real-valued vector with a small number of coordinates can, in principle, encode many distinctions in very fine numerical differences. In practice, finite precision, noise, limited data and the smoothness of learned functions constrain how reliably such distinctions can be used. Conversely, a very wide feature may carry highly redundant coordinates. Dimension is a proxy for capacity, while useful compression concerns which differences remain reliably decodable under the conditions where the model will be used.



[#unique_section2_ch3_025_projection_head_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== The projection head and the feature that survives

Many contrastive and bootstrap methods use a projection head between an encoder and the objective:

[latexmath]
++++
h=f_\theta(x),\qquad z=g_\phi(h).
\tag{3.13}
++++

The loss is calculated on latexmath:[z], while the representation evaluated downstream is often latexmath:[h]. A projection head gives the pretraining objective a dedicated space in which to impose its constraints. It does not follow that latexmath:[z] and latexmath:[h] have the same geometry or serve the same downstream purpose.

The exported tensor must be identified explicitly. Some evaluations use the feature latexmath:[h] before the projector; others use intermediate or patch-level transformer features. A low pretraining loss measured on latexmath:[z] is not itself evidence that the feature latexmath:[h] has the same geometry or usefulness. The projector can change the geometry substantially because it is a learned, generally nonlinear map. The pretraining loss directly constrains latexmath:[z], not every pairwise relation in latexmath:[h]. Claims about representation quality should state the tensor and evaluation protocol rather than infer quality from training loss alone.


[#unique_section2_ch3_026_global_local_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== A global vector or a grid of local features?

A global vector is compact and convenient for classification, retrieval and simple interfaces. A grid of patch features preserves approximate locations and correspondences. A temporal sequence preserves history only if its aggregation does not erase the changes needed by prediction. These forms are related but not interchangeable.

For patch features latexmath:[h_1,\ldots,h_P], simple average pooling forms

[latexmath]
++++
h_{\mathrm{global}}=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{i=1}^{P}h_i.
++++

This compresses the grid into one vector and removes explicit patch indexing from the output. Distinct spatial arrangements can therefore produce the same pooled vector. Retaining the grid preserves a feature for each patch and its token position, although the feature values themselves are not guaranteed to encode accurate geometry. The choice is a representational trade-off: a vector is compact, while a grid makes local correspondences accessible to later components. Global pooling is appropriate when spatial arrangement is irrelevant, but can be harmful when the target depends on location or correspondence between regions. A temporal representation also has to retain motion-related information when it changes the target future.

The pooling equation also gives a simple source of aliasing. Consider two feature grids whose patch vectors are identical as a multiset but appear in different spatial orders. Average pooling gives the same sum, hence the same global vector, even though the arrangements differ. If the target depends on the arrangement, the pooled representation cannot be sufficient for that target. Positional information elsewhere in the encoder can reduce such collisions, but the pooling operation itself does not preserve the index of each patch.



[#unique_section2_ch3_027_compression_sufficiency_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Compression and predictive sufficiency

A useful representation retains enough information for the relevant prediction while suppressing nuisance variation. There is no universally optimal latent dimension for all tasks. In some settings a small state may be sufficient; in others, spatial features, temporal context or multiple latent components may be necessary.

Here, *compact* means constrained and usable by downstream prediction, not necessarily information-theoretically minimal. The bottleneck should be selected and tested against the downstream task. A low-dimensional representation that aliases important states is not a success; a larger representation is not automatically better if it mostly stores nuisance detail.

A low self-supervised loss establishes only that the representation satisfies its training objective. It does not establish that the representation is useful for the quantity a downstream model must predict. The distinction is mathematical: each pretraining objective constrains a selected relation, whereas predictive sufficiency concerns the full conditional distribution of a specified target.

A linear probe tests whether a selected property is accessible through a linear map:

[latexmath]
++++
\hat y=Wz+b.
++++

A poor score does not prove the property is absent; the representation may encode it nonlinearly. A high score establishes linear accessibility under that probe and data split, not sufficiency for every possible target. Probe results should identify the feature tensor, normalization, readout family, training split and held-out evaluation set. Otherwise, a reported difference can reflect feature selection, readout capacity or data leakage rather than the representation itself.

Other diagnostics measure other things. Per-coordinate variance can detect dimensions that are constant across a batch. The covariance spectrum can reveal whether most feature variation lies in only a few directions. Nearest-neighbor retrieval measures local geometry under a selected distance. Reconstruction evaluates a particular target and decoder. Contrastive retrieval evaluates ranking among a sampled candidate set. These results are not interchangeable: none alone demonstrates that the representation preserves every distinction required to predict the future.

A useful formal definition is *predictive sufficiency*. Let latexmath:[X] denote the available observation or observation history, latexmath:[Z=f(X)] the representation, and latexmath:[Y] the future target. The representation is sufficient for latexmath:[Y] if

[latexmath]
++++
P(Y\mid X)=P(Y\mid Z).
++++

Equivalently, latexmath:[Y\perp X\mid Z]: after conditioning on latexmath:[Z], the full input provides no additional information about latexmath:[Y]. This definition is relative to the target distribution. A representation can be sufficient for a coarse category while not being sufficient for a precise location, or sufficient for a one-step target while losing information required for a longer horizon. There is no target-independent scalar score that certifies sufficiency in all these settings.

The failure caused by aliasing follows immediately. Suppose two inputs map to the same representation,

[latexmath]
++++
f(x_1)=f(x_2)=z,
\qquad P(Y\mid x_1)\ne P(Y\mid x_2).
++++

A predictor that receives only latexmath:[z] must produce the same output distribution for both inputs. It cannot equal both distinct conditional distributions. Increasing the capacity of the downstream predictor cannot restore information removed by the encoder.

Consider two short video histories that end with an object at the same image location. In one history it has been moving left; in the other it has been moving right. If the representation discards the temporal difference and maps both histories to the same latexmath:[z], their next-frame distributions may differ even though the last frames are identical. The example does not imply that velocity must always be explicitly represented as a coordinate. It shows that distinctions that change the target distribution cannot be merged without loss relative to that target.

The practical evaluation should therefore state the claim being tested. A probe supports a claim about accessibility of specified variables. A nearest-neighbor score supports a claim about local feature geometry under a specified metric. A reconstruction metric supports a claim about recovery of a chosen target. A predictive-sufficiency claim is stronger and requires evidence tied to the target distribution and observation history of interest.

[#unique_section_ch3_027_summary_ID,reftext={chapter}.{counter:section},caption='{chapter}.{section}']
== Summary

Self-supervised learning constructs a training target from data. Contrastive methods identify a positive among sampled candidates; their gradients depend on the candidate distribution, and their information-theoretic interpretation requires specific sampling assumptions. BYOL and SimSiam predict a detached feature target but differ in whether that target is updated by EMA. DINO matches teacher output distributions. Barlow Twins penalizes departures of cross-view correlation from identity, while VICReg separates view agreement, a variance floor and covariance regularization.

Masked prediction changes both the hidden context and the target: MAE predicts pixels, BEiT predicts discrete codes, and iBOT/data2vec use teacher-derived targets. The choice of target determines what errors are penalized. The latent bottleneck then determines which features are retained and which distinctions may be lost. A useful representation must preserve enough information for the prediction of interest; the pretraining loss by itself cannot establish that condition. SIGReg/LeJEPA and VISReg constrain different aspects of embedding distribution shape, but their guarantees remain tied to their stated theory and recipes.
