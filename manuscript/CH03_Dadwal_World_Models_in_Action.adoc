World Models in Action

[#unique_chapter3_title_ID,reftext={chapter},caption='{chapter}']
= Representation learning before generation

This chapter covers

* How self-supervised objectives define useful invariances
* Contrastive learning, bootstrap learning, redundancy reduction and self-distillation
* Masked prediction, target spaces and latent bottlenecks
* How to assess whether a representation retains the state needed for prediction

A world model needs an internal state that preserves distinctions relevant to predicting what happens next. Raw observations are often too high-dimensional and entangled to serve directly as a convenient state. An encoder transforms them into a representation, but the transformation cannot be judged by compression alone. It must retain the distinctions that a dynamics model or planner will need.

When labels are unavailable, the encoder still needs a learning signal. Self-supervised learning obtains that signal from the data itself: two views can be treated as related, one representation can predict another, feature statistics can be constrained, or missing content can be predicted from context. These procedures differ in what they reward the representation for retaining.

The central idea is that an objective is not a neutral way to optimize an encoder. It specifies a relationship the model must satisfy. The way that relationship is constructed determines which variations are ignored, which distinctions are amplified, and which information may be lost. No objective guarantees that its representation is a sufficient physical state. That claim requires separate evidence.

The chapter follows the book's TOC: self-supervised foundations, contrastive learning, masked prediction, latent bottlenecks, compact state representations and a simple encoder. BYOL, SimSiam, Barlow Twins, VICReg and the DINO family receive intuitive, mechanism-level explanations. DeepCluster, SwAV, CLIP, BERT and GPT are brief reference points, not separate tutorials. The examples explain learning mechanisms; they are not a ranking of methods trained with different data, architectures and budgets.

[#unique_section_ch3_001_the_objective_is_a_contract_ID,reftext={chapter}.{counter:section},caption='{chapter}.{section}']
== Self-supervised learning foundations

[sidebar]
.The central distinction
****
Self-supervised learning constructs a relationship from data and optimizes an encoder to satisfy it. That relationship may be useful without being sufficient for a world model. Separate the training target, the learned representation and evidence about downstream state quality.
****

[#unique_section2_ch3_002_representation_not_compression_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== A representation is not just a compressed observation

Let latexmath:[x] be an observation and let latexmath:[f_\theta] be an encoder with parameters latexmath:[\theta]. Its output is a vector latexmath:[z]:

[latexmath]
++++
z=f_\theta(x).
\tag{3.1}
++++

A smaller representation is useful only if the information removed is unnecessary for the task. A compact vector might preserve object identity but lose velocity. It might preserve average appearance but erase the visual cue that distinguishes an open gripper from a closed one. Compression creates the possibility of a useful state and the possibility of aliasing.

Chapter 2 formalized the desired contract in terms of sufficiency, minimality and aliasing. Self-supervised objectives do not observe that contract directly. They rely on a proxy relationship in the data. A representation may perform well on the proxy task while failing to retain information the world model needs.

[#unique_section2_ch3_003_invariance_is_a_decision_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Invariance is a decision

Suppose latexmath:[T_1] and latexmath:[T_2] are random transformations applied to the same input:

[latexmath]
++++
x_1=T_1(x),\qquad x_2=T_2(x).
\tag{3.2}
++++

A view-consistency objective encourages latexmath:[f_\theta(x_1)] and latexmath:[f_\theta(x_2)] to agree. The design decision is hidden in latexmath:[T_1] and latexmath:[T_2]: they define which input differences the model is encouraged to ignore.

Random crops are useful when the cropped-out region is irrelevant to the intended representation. They can be harmful when the missing region contains a control-relevant object or a velocity cue. Color jitter can suppress nuisance lighting changes, but it can also remove a visual marker used for localization. The transformation is part of the model's inductive bias, not a neutral augmentation step.

An ideal invariance would identify observations that the downstream task truly treats as equivalent. A practical augmentation only approximates that relation. We should not assume that two views are interchangeable for every task just because they came from the same image.

Historically, visual self-supervision used hand-designed pretext tasks such as predicting image rotation (Gidaris et al., 2018), restoring the spatial order of jigsaw patches (Noroozi and Favaro, 2016), predicting spatial context (Doersch et al., 2015), and colorizing grayscale images (Zhang et al., 2016). These tasks assign synthetic targets to unlabeled data and make the target-design assumption easy to see. They remain useful conceptual examples, but the architecture survey below focuses on the broader contrastive, bootstrap, statistical, clustering and masked-prediction families that followed.

[sidebar]
.The principle behind representation learning
****
Every self-supervised method constructs a target relationship from the data. The relationship may be agreement between views, a ranking among candidates, a teacher's output distribution, statistics of a feature batch, a cluster assignment or a missing patch. Ask what information makes that relationship easy to satisfy, then ask whether that information is what the downstream model needs.
****

[#unique_section2_ch3_010_byol_and_simsiam_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== BYOL and SimSiam: bootstrap prediction

BYOL (Grill et al., 2020) uses an online encoder and projection head, an extra predictor on the online branch, and a target encoder with a target projection head. The target branch is stop-gradient, and its parameters are an exponential moving average of the online parameters. A normalized regression loss asks the online prediction to match the target representation of another view. Training can also swap the roles of the two views.

[NOTE]
====
**Figure brief — artwork pending; do not insert an image yet.**

SimSiam's stop-gradient differs from BYOL's EMA update

Show shared encoder weights on both view paths, a predictor on the online path and a stop-gradient symbol after the target representation. Show gradients only through the online side for that loss term. Do not draw a target encoder or EMA arrow.

**Official/source reference:** Chen and He (2021), Exploring Simple Siamese Representation Learning, and facebookresearch/simsiam. Redraw as an original schematic in the Manning figure style; do not paste a paper screenshot.
====

SimSiam (Chen and He, 2021) keeps the predictor and stop-gradient asymmetry but removes the moving-average target update. Both branches share encoder parameters; the detached target branch does not receive gradient through that loss term. The important operation is not just two encoders that agree. It is which pathway receives gradients and how the predictor and normalizations affect parameter updates.

A constant representation can make a simple view-agreement loss zero. Thus neither predictor asymmetry nor EMA should be presented as a standalone mathematical theorem that collapse is impossible in all settings. A collapsed solution may exist yet be unstable under a particular training dynamics; an empirical model may avoid collapse for a particular recipe without a universal proof. Distinguish the existence of a collapsed solution, its stability, and the outcome of a particular run.

[#unique_section2_ch3_011_dino_and_self_distillation_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== DINO: match a teacher's distribution

DINO (Caron et al., 2021) replaces direct feature regression with distribution matching. A student and a teacher map different views to probability distributions over learned prototype outputs. The student is trained by cross-entropy to match a teacher distribution that has been centered and sharpened. The teacher parameters follow an EMA of the student parameters.

Centering and sharpening have different roles: centering modifies output statistics to resist concentration on a small set of prototypes; sharpening lowers the entropy of the teacher's target distribution. Prototype indices are learned output coordinates, not automatically human-defined categories. DINO is best understood as self-distillation with a prototype distribution, rather than as an ordinary clustering algorithm.

Multi-crop training supplies a useful extra constraint. The teacher receives the larger global crops, while the student also sees smaller local crops. A local crop usually lacks some of the global context, so its output must match a target produced from a different field of view. This encourages some features to remain consistent across scales. It does not mean that every local crop contains enough evidence to infer every property of the entire scene; the target and crop protocol define what consistency is encouraged. The prototype outputs are learned coordinates, not labels assigned by a person, and their semantics must be established through evaluation.

DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2023) and DINOv3 (Siméoni et al., 2025) demonstrate that representation quality depends on more than the named loss. Data curation, model and data scale, architecture, resolution, schedule and training diagnostics are all part of the system. DINOv3, introduced in 2025, emphasizes scalable dense features and introduces Gram anchoring to address degradation of feature maps during extended training. That development is a reminder that global image representations and high-quality local features are different objectives to monitor.

[#unique_section2_ch3_013_barlow_twins_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Barlow Twins: cross-correlation toward identity

Let latexmath:[Z^A] and latexmath:[Z^B] be feature batches from two views. Normalize each feature dimension across the batch and define cross-correlation latexmath:[C]:

[latexmath]
++++
C_{ij}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\tilde z^A_{b,i}\tilde z^B_{b,j}.
\tag{3.3}
++++

If corresponding dimensions agree and distinct dimensions are uncorrelated across views, latexmath:[C] approaches the identity matrix. Barlow Twins minimizes the diagonal mismatch and penalizes off-diagonal cross-correlation:

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{BT}}=\sum_i(1-C_{ii})^2+
\lambda\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}C_{ij}^{2}.
\tag{3.4}
++++

The diagonal terms align a feature with itself across views; the off-diagonal terms discourage redundant correlations between different dimensions. This is a second-order statistical objective. An identity cross-correlation matrix does not prove that features are statistically independent under arbitrary distributions, and it does not prove that each coordinate has a useful physical interpretation.

[#unique_section2_ch3_014_vicreg_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== VICReg: make three constraints explicit

VICReg separates invariance, variance and covariance. For two feature batches latexmath:[Z] and latexmath:[Z'], the invariance term is a mean-squared difference:

[latexmath]
++++
s(Z,Z')=\frac{1}{N}\lVert Z-Z'\rVert_F^2.
\tag{3.5}
++++

For each feature dimension latexmath:[j], let latexmath:[\sigma_j(Z)] be its standard deviation over the batch. A variance penalty puts a floor under each dimension's standard deviation:

[latexmath]
++++
v(Z)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_j\max(0,\gamma-\sigma_j(Z)).
\tag{3.6}
++++

The covariance term penalizes the squared off-diagonal entries of the empirical covariance matrix:

[latexmath]
++++
c(Z)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i\ne j}\operatorname{Cov}(Z)_{ij}^{2}.
\tag{3.7}
++++

The full objective is a weighted sum of the invariance term and the corresponding variance and covariance terms for both views. The published implementation's coefficients and numerical choices matter; the equations state the structural roles.

The reduction convention is important when reading code. The paper's invariance term averages the squared vector norm over examples, whereas the companion's compact implementation uses elementwise mean-squared error, which also averages over feature dimensions. Those differ by a factor of the embedding dimension. Do not copy coefficients from one reduction convention to the other without rescaling.

If every example has the same value in one dimension, its batch standard deviation is zero and the variance penalty is positive for any positive margin. That makes the anti-collapse constraint explicit. It does not prove that the surviving variation is semantically relevant. A feature can vary with background texture and still satisfy a variance floor.

The methods are related but not identical. Barlow Twins uses normalized cross-correlation as the central matrix objective. VICReg uses a direct invariance term, a standard-deviation floor and a covariance penalty. Their shared lesson is that view agreement and non-collapse are separate requirements. Their moment constraints are not a guarantee of a world-model-ready state.

=== Other methods in brief

**DeepCluster** alternates offline clustering of current features with training on the resulting pseudo-labels. **SwAV** forms prototype assignments online and predicts assignments across views, using a balancing step. These methods differ mainly in how their pseudo-targets are generated and refreshed.

**CLIP** aligns paired images and text with contrastive learning. **BERT** predicts masked tokens from bidirectional context; **GPT-style causal modeling** predicts the next token from preceding tokens. These examples show that self-supervised targets can use natural pairings or sequence structure, not just augmented images. Their details are outside this chapter's main derivations.

[#unique_section2_ch3_022_frontier_and_scope_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Hand-off to predictive representations

DINOv3 illustrates the recent effort to scale self-distilled visual features while preserving dense spatial information. The method's reported Gram anchoring addresses degradation of dense features during long training schedules. This is not just DINO with more parameters: data curation, architecture, optimization and feature diagnostics contribute to the system.

Joint-embedding predictive architectures are another important direction. I-JEPA (Assran et al., 2023) predicts representations of target image regions from a context representation. V-JEPA (Bardes et al., 2024) moves the target into video, and V-JEPA 2 (Assran et al., 2025) explores video representation learning for visual understanding and anticipation. V-JEPA 2-AC adds action-conditioned post-training for robotic control. These are distinct stages. Passive video pretraining alone does not specify how the world changes under an action.

As of October 2026, V-JEPA 2.1 represents another step toward dense predictive features, but its context/target construction belongs in Chapter 4.

The JEPA family is the next conceptual step: predict target representations in embedding space rather than reconstruct every missing pixel. Chapter 4 owns the detailed treatment of I-JEPA, V-JEPA, V-JEPA 2, LeJEPA, context/target design and action-conditioned prediction. Chapter 3 only needs to establish why target choice matters.

Diffusion and flow-matching objectives learn generative processes; their substantive treatment belongs in Chapters 8 and 9, not in this representation-encoder survey.

[#unique_section2_ch3_005_from_pairs_to_candidate_classification_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== From pairs to candidate classification

A contrastive method begins with an anchor, a related positive example and distractors called negatives. The encoder maps each example to a feature vector. A similarity function scores the anchor against each candidate. Training rewards the positive for receiving a higher score than the negatives.

For two feature vectors, cosine similarity is their dot product after unit normalization:

[latexmath]
++++
s(z_i,z_j)=\frac{z_i^\top z_j}{\lVert z_i\rVert_2\lVert z_j\rVert_2}.
\tag{3.8}
++++

A temperature latexmath:[\tau>0] scales the similarity before applying softmax. A lower temperature makes the candidate distribution sharper and increases the effect of high-scoring candidates. It also changes gradient scale and optimization sensitivity.

Suppose a batch contains latexmath:[N] source images. Two stochastic views of each image give latexmath:[2N] encoded vectors. For anchor latexmath:[i], let latexmath:[p(i)] be the index of its other view. Excluding the anchor itself, every remaining view is a candidate. The NT-Xent loss for that anchor is

[latexmath]
++++
\ell_i=-\log
\frac{\exp(s(z_i,z_{p(i)})/\tau)}
{\sum_{a=1}^{2N}\mathbf{1}[a\ne i]\exp(s(z_i,z_a)/\tau)}.
\tag{3.9}
++++

The total loss averages over the anchors. In the denominator are one positive and latexmath:[2N-2] negatives. The positive is included in the denominator; the anchor is not. Stating the candidate set explicitly avoids ambiguity because papers do not all use the same symbol for the number of candidates or negatives.

This objective is ordinary softmax cross-entropy. Write latexmath:[a_{ij}=s(z_i,z_j)/\tau], and let latexmath:[q_{ij}] be the softmax probability assigned to candidate latexmath:[j]. Differentiating with respect to each candidate logit gives

[latexmath]
++++
\frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial a_{ij}}
=q_{ij}-\mathbf{1}[j=p(i)].
\tag{3.10}
++++

The positive logit receives gradient latexmath:[q_{i,p(i)}-1], which pushes it upward under gradient descent. A negative logit receives gradient latexmath:[q_{ij}], which pushes it downward. The gradient on a negative is weighted by its current probability: a hard negative receives more repulsive gradient than an already dissimilar candidate. Since latexmath:[a_{ij}=s(z_i,z_j)/\tau], differentiation with respect to similarity introduces the factor latexmath:[1/\tau].

[#unique_section2_ch3_006_what_infoNCE_does_and_does_not_prove_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== What InfoNCE does and does not prove

The denominator makes the objective relative: the model must distinguish the designated positive from sampled competitors. Maximizing positive similarity alone would not impose that ranking constraint. With unnormalized dot products it can also reward increasing feature norms; unit normalization bounds the score but does not remove dependence on the sampling protocol.

The labels “positive” and “negative” come from the data construction. A negative may be an image of the same semantic category as the anchor. This is called a false negative relative to that semantic task. It is not an algebraic error: it is a mismatch between the sampling label and the relationship we want the representation to encode. Augmentations create a related risk. If a transformation destroys a relevant feature, the loss may teach the encoder to ignore that feature.

InfoNCE is often connected to mutual information. For a specified joint distribution of positive pairs and an appropriate negative-sampling distribution, the optimal critic is related to the density ratio between joint and independent samples. Under those assumptions, a commonly used form of the bound is

[latexmath]
++++
I(X;Y)\ge \log K-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}},
\tag{3.11}
++++

where latexmath:[K] denotes the number of candidates under the convention used by the derivation. Candidate-sampling assumptions are essential. A loss called InfoNCE does not automatically produce this bound under every arbitrary negative-sampling scheme.

The bound also has a ceiling of latexmath:[\log K] for finite candidate count. Increasing mutual information beyond that ceiling cannot tighten this particular bound without increasing latexmath:[K]. More fundamentally, maximizing dependence between views is not identical to retaining information needed for a downstream physical prediction. The chapter therefore derives the classification objective and its gradient first; the information-theoretic interpretation is useful, but it is not a universal guarantee of representation quality.

[#unique_section2_ch3_007_simclr_and_moco_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== SimCLR and MoCo: two ways to construct candidates

SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020) made the augmented-view recipe explicit. Two views pass through an encoder latexmath:[f] and a nonlinear projection head latexmath:[g]. The contrastive objective is applied to the projected vectors, while the encoder representation is typically what downstream tasks use. That distinction matters: the loss space and the feature space are not necessarily the same. SimCLR's results also depended on augmentation composition, batch size, architecture, training duration and optimization. Its contribution is a clear and strong recipe, not a proof that every contrastive method needs one specific batch size or projection head.

MoCo (He et al., 2020) addressed a different engineering issue: keeping a large candidate dictionary without requiring every negative to come from the current batch. It uses a query encoder and a key encoder. The key encoder follows an exponential moving average:

[latexmath]
++++
\theta_k\leftarrow m\theta_k+(1-m)\theta_q.
\tag{3.12}
++++

Encoded keys are stored in a queue. A query can therefore compare with many keys produced by earlier minibatches. Since queued keys become stale as parameters change, the slowly moving key encoder reduces, but does not eliminate, dictionary inconsistency. MoCo v2 (Chen et al., 2020) strengthens the training recipe; MoCo v3 (Chen et al., 2021) studies a changed recipe for vision transformers. These developments should be described with their actual architecture and training context, not as arbitrary version numbers.

Both methods remain sensitive to the definition of positive pairs and negatives. A queue solves the size and update-consistency problem; it does not establish that every negative is semantically different.

[#unique_section2_ch3_008_cpc_as_a_historical_bridge_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== CPC as a historical bridge

Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC; van den Oord et al., 2018) encodes a sequence and uses a context vector from the past to score future latent representations. An InfoNCE-style objective teaches the context to identify the observed future latent among candidate latents. CPC is a useful historical bridge between contrastive discrimination and predictive representations, but it is not the centerpiece of a contemporary visual SSL survey.

A CPC future-latent retrieval score is not the same as an action-conditioned dynamics model. If the predictor has no action input, it does not specify which action caused the future. Successful retrieval does not establish recursive physical rollout accuracy, Markov sufficiency or planning value. Those are separate claims requiring separate tests.

== Masked prediction: choose what the model must recover

[#unique_section2_ch3_017_one_mask_many_targets_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== One mask, several target spaces

Suppose an input is divided into visible positions latexmath:[V] and masked positions latexmath:[M]. An encoder processes the allowed context and a prediction head estimates a target latexmath:[y_i] at masked positions:

[latexmath]
++++
L=\frac{1}{|M|}\sum_{i\in M}\ell(\hat y_i,y_i).
\tag{3.13}
++++

This form leaves the crucial choice open: what is the target? It might be pixel values, a discrete code, a word token, a replaced-token label or a contextualized feature. The mask tells the model where information is withheld; the target tells it what relationship must be learned.

[#unique_section2_ch3_018_mae_pixels_and_asymmetry_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== MAE: predict masked pixels

A masked autoencoder (MAE; He et al., 2022) hides a large fraction of image patches and sends only visible patches through the encoder. A smaller decoder receives the encoded visible patches plus mask tokens and predicts patch pixels; the loss is applied to masked patches. This asymmetric design reduces encoder computation because masked patches do not pass through the expensive encoder.

Under squared error, the conditional mean minimizes expected prediction risk. When several patch completions are plausible from visible context, that mean can average them. The limitation is not that pixel reconstruction is always poor; it is that reconstructing pixels does not guarantee the encoder preserves the state variables a downstream model needs. The encoder may be retained for transfer while the reconstruction decoder is discarded.

[#unique_section2_ch3_019_beit_ibot_and_data2vec_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== BEiT, iBOT and data2vec: change the target

BEiT (Bao et al., 2022) predicts discrete visual-token IDs produced by a learned tokenizer. Rather than regress pixel values, the model classifies the tokenizer's code for each masked patch. The tokenizer introduces its own prior: useful structure can be learned from the code vocabulary, but tokenizer errors and biases enter the target.

iBOT (Zhou et al., 2022) combines masked patch prediction with teacher-student self-distillation. It is an example where a family label is insufficient: the input is masked, target distributions come from a teacher, and prototype outputs structure those targets. Other masked-image methods deliberately change the target: SimMIM (Xie et al., 2022) uses a simple pixel-reconstruction objective; MaskFeat (Wei et al., 2022) predicts hand-designed visual features such as HOG rather than raw pixels; BEiT v2 improves the discrete-target route with semantic visual tokenization. These variations reinforce the same principle: the mask alone does not define the learning task.

data2vec (Baevski et al., 2022) predicts contextualized teacher features from masked input and was designed as a framework spanning speech, vision and language. A latent target may contain broader context than a local pixel target, but matching a teacher does not automatically ensure that the feature preserves all future-relevant or causal state information. In every case, the representation is shaped by the selected target.

BERT-style masked language modeling predicts selected corrupted tokens from bidirectional context. GPT-style causal language modeling predicts the next token from preceding tokens only. ELECTRA trains a discriminator to detect replaced tokens. These are brief analogies for target/context construction, not additional implementation tutorials.

[#unique_section_ch3_024_latent_bottlenecks_ID,reftext={chapter}.{counter:section},caption='{chapter}.{section}']
== Latent bottlenecks

[#unique_section2_ch3_024_bottleneck_dimension_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Dimension is capacity, not useful information

A latent bottleneck maps a high-dimensional observation or token grid into a more constrained representation. The simplest form is a vector latexmath:[h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}]. Lowering latexmath:[d] limits the number of coordinates available, but it does not directly measure how much task-relevant information remains. A small vector may preserve the few variables needed by a task; a wide vector may devote many dimensions to lighting, textures, camera identity or other nuisance variation.

The important question is not “how small can the vector be?” It is “what information can be discarded without merging states whose future predictions should differ?” This is task-relative. An object classifier may ignore location, while a visual controller may need position, orientation and contact. A representation useful for matching images may be insufficient for predicting motion.

[#unique_section2_ch3_025_projection_head_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== The projection head and the feature that survives

Many contrastive and bootstrap methods use a projection head between an encoder and the objective:

[latexmath]
++++
h=f_\theta(x),\qquad z=g_\phi(h).
\tag{3.14}
++++

The loss is calculated on latexmath:[z], while the representation evaluated downstream is often latexmath:[h]. A projection head gives the pretraining objective a dedicated space in which to impose its constraints. It does not follow that latexmath:[z] and latexmath:[h] have the same geometry or serve the same downstream purpose.

The exact export point is method- and evaluation-specific. Some studies report features before the projector; others use intermediate or patch-level transformer features. The manuscript should state which tensor is evaluated. A pretraining loss measured on latexmath:[z] must not be described as a direct measure of the quality of latexmath:[h].

[#unique_section2_ch3_026_global_local_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== A global vector or a grid of local features?

A global vector is compact and convenient for classification, retrieval and simple interfaces. A grid of patch features preserves approximate locations and correspondences. A temporal sequence preserves history only if its aggregation does not erase the changes needed by prediction. These forms are related but not interchangeable.

Global pooling can intentionally discard spatial layout. That may be appropriate for recognizing an object regardless of position but harmful for tracking, localization or action selection. A patch grid retains more spatial structure at the cost of memory and downstream compute. A temporal state can incorporate history but must preserve velocity, contact or other dynamics-relevant quantities when they matter.

[#unique_section2_ch3_027_compression_sufficiency_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Compression and predictive sufficiency

A useful representation retains enough information for the relevant prediction while suppressing nuisance variation. There is no universally optimal latent dimension for all tasks. In some settings a small state may be sufficient; in others, spatial features, a history window, proprioception or multiple latent components may be necessary.

Here, *compact* means constrained and usable by downstream dynamics or planning, not necessarily information-theoretically minimal. The bottleneck should be selected and tested against the downstream task. A low-dimensional representation that aliases important states is not a success; a larger representation is not automatically better if it mostly stores nuisance detail.

[#unique_section_ch3_026_a_world_model_audit_ID,reftext={chapter}.{counter:section},caption='{chapter}.{section}']
== Compact state representations

A low self-supervised loss answers whether the representation satisfies its training objective. It does not answer whether the representation is a useful state. A linear probe can reveal whether selected variables are linearly decodable, but cannot certify that all future-relevant information is present. A reconstruction score can show whether target pixels are predicted, but cannot certify causal state. A future-latent retrieval score tests candidate identification under the chosen sampling protocol, not necessarily physical rollout or action-conditioned prediction.

Use a sequence of checks whose claims are explicit:

. **Inspect the view transformations.** Identify what information may be erased by crops, color changes, masking or temporal sampling.
. **Inspect the target.** Determine whether it is a candidate ID, teacher feature, statistic, cluster assignment, token or pixel. Ask which information lowers its loss.
. **Check collapse directly.** Monitor per-dimension standard deviations, covariance/correlation structure, feature rank and distribution across examples. A scalar training loss is not enough.
. **Test relevant variables.** Use held-out labels or simulator state only for evaluation; state clearly which variables and data splits the probe uses.
. **Test the distinction required by prediction.** For world models, include occlusion, velocity, changing geometry and relevant context where possible. Distinguish single-frame representation quality from sequence prediction.
. **Test action conditioning and downstream use separately.** A model that predicts a passive future distribution need not know which future follows a selected action. Dynamics and planning require their own tests.

[sidebar]
.Three components, three different jobs
****
A static visual encoder maps a frame, or a fixed stack of frames, to a latent vector. An optional recurrent wrapper carries a learned state across observations. Neither is a complete world model. A full world model additionally needs a transition model that predicts future state or observations conditioned on actions, and a method for evaluating or planning over candidate futures.
****

The correct conclusion is narrower than a model-card claim: a method learns the information that makes its constructed target relationship easier to satisfy, subject to architecture and optimization. Whether that information is useful for a particular agent is a downstream question.

. **Source-inspected implementation.** The code is read to identify the actual forward path, loss reduction, target update and recipe. This supports statements about that exact pinned source.

[#unique_section_ch3_027_summary_ID,reftext={chapter}.{counter:section},caption='{chapter}.{section}']
== Summary

Self-supervised learning constructs a learning signal from data. Contrastive objectives turn positive-pair matching into candidate classification. SimCLR and MoCo show different ways to build the candidate set. BYOL, SimSiam and DINO use prediction or teacher distributions without explicit negative pairs, but agreement alone is compatible with collapse. Barlow Twins and VICReg make redundancy and variance constraints explicit. DeepCluster and SwAV use pseudo-labels or prototypes. Masked prediction shows that the target can be a pixel, discrete code, word, replaced-token label or contextualized feature.

Across methods, the same questions recur: which inputs are treated as equivalent, what target is predicted, which context is available, how the objective handles degenerate solutions, and what evaluation supports the intended claim. Modern methods improve data, targets, architecture and training recipes in different ways; no single loss is a universal guarantee of a sufficient physical state.

For world modeling, representation learning is only one part of the system. A good state must retain distinctions that matter for future prediction. The encoder's self-supervised loss must not be confused with an action-conditioned dynamics objective or evidence of planning utility.
