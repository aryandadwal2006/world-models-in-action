World Models in Action

[#unique_chapter3_title_ID,reftext={chapter},caption='{chapter}']
= Representation learning before generation

This chapter covers

* The assumptions built into self-supervised objectives
* Contrastive, bootstrap, self-distillation and redundancy-reduction objectives
* Masked prediction and the choice of prediction target
* Latent bottlenecks and the limits of representation evaluation

A world model needs a representation that preserves distinctions relevant to predicting future observations. Pixels contain both useful information and details that a predictive model may not need. An encoder maps the observation \(x\) to a representation \(z=f_\theta(x)\), but compression alone does not make \(z\) useful. Two observations that should lead to different predictions must not be merged merely because their images are similar.

When labels are unavailable, the training signal must be constructed from the data. Methods may identify a matching view among candidates, predict a representation produced by another branch, constrain feature statistics across a batch, or predict content hidden from the encoder. These objectives differ in their targets, their assumptions and their optimization dynamics.

The central question is therefore not just whether the training loss decreases. It is what the loss rewards the encoder for preserving. A feature may solve the pretraining task yet omit information needed for a later prediction. The following sections derive the principal objectives and explain exactly what their mathematical constraints do and do not establish.
[#unique_section_ch3_001_the_objective_is_a_contract_ID,reftext={chapter}.{counter:section},caption='{chapter}.{section}']
== Self-supervised learning foundations

[sidebar]
.The central distinction
****
Self-supervised learning constructs a relationship from data and optimizes an encoder to satisfy it. That relationship may be useful without being sufficient for a world model. Separate the training target, the learned representation and evidence about downstream state quality.
****

[#unique_section2_ch3_002_representation_not_compression_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== A representation is not just a compressed observation

Let latexmath:[x] be an observation and let latexmath:[f_\theta] be an encoder with parameters latexmath:[\theta]. Its output is a vector latexmath:[z]:

[latexmath]
++++
z=f_\theta(x).
\tag{3.1}
++++

A smaller representation is useful only if the information removed is unnecessary for the task. A vector may preserve object identity while losing location or orientation. It may describe a frame's appearance while losing motion that is apparent only from a sequence. Compression can remove nuisance detail, but it can also merge observations whose futures differ.

Chapter 2 formalized the desired contract in terms of sufficiency, minimality and aliasing. Self-supervised objectives do not observe that contract directly. They rely on a proxy relationship in the data. A representation may perform well on the proxy task while failing to retain information the world model needs.

[#unique_section2_ch3_003_invariance_is_a_decision_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Invariance is a decision

Suppose latexmath:[T_1] and latexmath:[T_2] are random transformations applied to the same input:

[latexmath]
++++
x_1=T_1(x),\qquad x_2=T_2(x).
\tag{3.2}
++++

A view-consistency objective encourages latexmath:[f_\theta(x_1)] and latexmath:[f_\theta(x_2)] to agree. The design decision is hidden in latexmath:[T_1] and latexmath:[T_2]: they define which input differences the model is encouraged to ignore.

Random crops are useful when the cropped-out region is irrelevant to the intended representation. They are harmful when the missing region contains information needed to distinguish the scene or predict its next state. Color jitter can suppress nuisance lighting changes, but it can also remove a visual cue on which a later prediction depends. The transformation is part of the learning problem, not a neutral preprocessing step.

An ideal invariance would identify observations that the downstream task truly treats as equivalent. A practical augmentation only approximates that relation. We should not assume that two views are interchangeable for every task just because they came from the same image.

Historically, visual self-supervision used hand-designed pretext tasks such as predicting image rotation (Gidaris et al., 2018), restoring the spatial order of jigsaw patches (Noroozi and Favaro, 2016), predicting spatial context (Doersch et al., 2015), and colorizing grayscale images (Zhang et al., 2016). These tasks assign synthetic targets to unlabeled data and make the target-design assumption easy to see. They remain useful conceptual examples, but the architecture survey below focuses on the broader contrastive, bootstrap, statistical, clustering and masked-prediction families that followed.

[sidebar]
.The principle behind representation learning
****
Every self-supervised method constructs a target relationship from the data. The relationship may be agreement between views, a ranking among candidates, a teacher's output distribution, statistics of a feature batch, a cluster assignment or a missing patch. Ask what information makes that relationship easy to satisfy, then ask whether that information is what the downstream model needs.
****

== Contrastive learning


[#unique_section2_ch3_005_from_pairs_to_candidate_classification_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== From pairs to candidate classification

A contrastive method begins with an anchor, a related positive example and distractors called negatives. The encoder maps each example to a feature vector. A similarity function scores the anchor against each candidate. Training rewards the positive for receiving a higher score than the negatives.

For two feature vectors, cosine similarity is their dot product after unit normalization:

[latexmath]
++++
s(z_i,z_j)=\frac{z_i^\top z_j}{\lVert z_i\rVert_2\lVert z_j\rVert_2}.
\tag{3.3}
++++

A temperature latexmath:[\tau>0] scales the similarity before applying softmax. A lower temperature makes the candidate distribution sharper and increases the effect of high-scoring candidates. It also changes gradient scale and optimization sensitivity.

Suppose a batch contains latexmath:[N] source images. Two stochastic views of each image give latexmath:[2N] encoded vectors. For anchor latexmath:[i], let latexmath:[p(i)] be the index of its other view. Excluding the anchor itself, every remaining view is a candidate. The NT-Xent loss for that anchor is

[latexmath]
++++
\ell_i=-\log
\frac{\exp(s(z_i,z_{p(i)})/\tau)}
{\sum_{a=1}^{2N}\mathbf{1}[a\ne i]\exp(s(z_i,z_a)/\tau)}.
\tag{3.4}
++++

The total loss averages over the anchors. In the denominator are one positive and latexmath:[2N-2] negatives. The positive is included in the denominator; the anchor is not. Stating the candidate set explicitly avoids ambiguity because papers do not all use the same symbol for the number of candidates or negatives.

This objective is ordinary softmax cross-entropy. Write latexmath:[a_{ij}=s(z_i,z_j)/\tau], and let latexmath:[q_{ij}] be the softmax probability assigned to candidate latexmath:[j]. Differentiating with respect to each candidate logit gives

[latexmath]
++++
\frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial a_{ij}}
=q_{ij}-\mathbf{1}[j=p(i)].
\tag{3.5}
++++

The positive logit receives gradient latexmath:[q_{i,p(i)}-1], which pushes it upward under gradient descent. A negative logit receives gradient latexmath:[q_{ij}], which pushes it downward. The gradient on a negative is weighted by its current probability: a hard negative receives more repulsive gradient than an already dissimilar candidate. Since \(a_{ij}=s(z_i,z_j)/\tau\), differentiation with respect to the unscaled similarity introduces the factor \(1/\tau\).

For example, suppose one anchor has cosine similarities \(0.80\), \(0.10\) and \(-0.30\) to its positive and two negatives, with \(\tau=0.5\). The logits are \(1.6\), \(0.2\) and \(-0.6\). Softmax assigns the positive probability about \(0.737\), so the loss for this anchor is \(-\log(0.737)\approx0.306\). If all three candidates received equal probability, the loss would be \(-\log(1/3)\approx1.099\). The loss measures whether the correct candidate is easy to identify relative to the candidates that happened to be sampled.

[#unique_section2_ch3_006_what_infoNCE_does_and_does_not_prove_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== What InfoNCE does and does not prove

The denominator makes the objective relative: the model must distinguish the designated positive from sampled competitors. Maximizing positive similarity alone would not impose that ranking constraint. With unnormalized dot products it can also reward increasing feature norms; unit normalization bounds the score but does not remove dependence on the sampling protocol.

The labels “positive” and “negative” come from the data construction. A negative may be an image of the same semantic category as the anchor. This is called a false negative relative to that semantic task. It is not an algebraic error: it is a mismatch between the sampling label and the relationship we want the representation to encode. Augmentations create a related risk. If a transformation destroys a relevant feature, the loss may teach the encoder to ignore that feature.

InfoNCE is also connected to mutual information. Let positive pairs come from a joint distribution \(p(x,y)\), and suppose negatives are sampled independently from the marginal \(p(y)\). The optimal scoring function for identifying the positive is proportional to the density ratio \(p(y\mid x)/p(y)\). Under these sampling assumptions, the InfoNCE loss with \(K\) total candidates gives the bound

[latexmath]
++++
I(X;Y)\ge \log K-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}}.
++++

The candidate count convention matters: here \(K\) includes the positive. The bound is limited by \(\log K\), so a finite candidate set cannot certify arbitrarily large mutual information. More importantly, real training pipelines may violate the independent-negative assumption, for example when observations are related or repeated. The bound is not a general guarantee of feature quality, and mutual information between two views is not the same as sufficiency for a later prediction.

[#unique_section2_ch3_007_simclr_and_moco_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== SimCLR and MoCo: two ways to construct candidates

SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020) made the augmented-view recipe explicit. Two views pass through an encoder latexmath:[f] and a nonlinear projection head latexmath:[g]. The contrastive objective is applied to the projected vectors, while the encoder representation is typically what downstream tasks use. That distinction matters: the loss space and the feature space are not necessarily the same. SimCLR's results also depended on augmentation composition, batch size, architecture, training duration and optimization. Its contribution is a clear and strong recipe, not a proof that every contrastive method needs one specific batch size or projection head.

MoCo (He et al., 2020) uses a query encoder and a key encoder, with a queue storing keys from earlier minibatches. This gives each query a larger candidate set than the current batch alone. The key encoder follows an exponential moving average:

[latexmath]
++++
\theta_k\leftarrow m\theta_k+(1-m)\theta_q.
\tag{3.6}
++++

The queue contains keys produced by earlier parameter values. They become stale as training proceeds; a slowly moving key encoder reduces abrupt drift in their representation space but cannot make historical keys identical to current ones. MoCo v2 (Chen et al., 2020) changed the training recipe, while MoCo v3 (Chen et al., 2021) studied a different recipe for vision transformers.

Both methods remain sensitive to the definition of positive pairs and negatives. A queue solves the size and update-consistency problem; it does not establish that every negative is semantically different.


[#unique_section2_ch3_008_cpc_as_a_historical_bridge_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== CPC as a historical bridge

Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC; van den Oord et al., 2018) encodes a sequence and uses a context vector from the past to score future latent representations. An InfoNCE-style objective teaches the context to identify the observed future latent among candidate latents. CPC is a useful historical bridge between contrastive discrimination and predictive representations, but it is not the centerpiece of a contemporary visual SSL survey.

A CPC future-latent retrieval score is not the same as an action-conditioned dynamics model. If the predictor has no action input, it does not specify which action caused the future. Successful retrieval does not establish recursive physical rollout accuracy, Markov sufficiency or planning value. Those are separate claims requiring separate tests.

[#unique_section_ch3_005_noncontrastive_objectives_ID,reftext={chapter}.{counter:section},caption='{chapter}.{section}']
== Non-contrastive objectives

Contrastive learning makes a designated positive score higher than sampled negatives. Non-contrastive methods remove that explicit competition, but they still need a mechanism that prevents agreement from becoming trivial. BYOL and SimSiam use asymmetric prediction pathways; DINO matches teacher distributions; Barlow Twins and VICReg constrain batch statistics.

[#unique_section2_ch3_030_agreement_and_collapse_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Why agreement alone admits collapse

Suppose two views of the same input are trained only to produce equal representations. A simple invariance loss is

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{inv}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert z_i-z_i'\rVert_2^2.
++++

If the encoder maps every input to the same constant vector \(c\), then \(z_i=z_i'=c\) for every example and the loss is zero. The objective has no term requiring different inputs to remain distinguishable. A constant output is therefore a collapsed solution, even though it is useless for representing the data.

This observation does not mean every method that matches two views will collapse. It means agreement alone cannot rule collapse out. The architecture, stop-gradient placement, target updates, normalizations and optimization dynamics affect which solutions training reaches. The methods below differ in precisely those mechanisms; none should be described as having a universal guarantee based only on its loss formula.

[#unique_section2_ch3_010_byol_and_simsiam_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== BYOL and SimSiam: bootstrap prediction

BYOL (Grill et al., 2020) has an online encoder, a projection head and a predictor, alongside a target encoder and target projection head. Only the online network receives gradients. The target network is updated by an exponential moving average (EMA) of the online encoder and projector; it is not updated by backpropagation.

Let \(z_1=g_\phi(f_\theta(x_1))\), \(p_1=q_\psi(z_1)\), and \(\bar z_2=g_{\bar\phi}(f_{\bar\theta}(x_2))\). The prediction loss for one direction is the normalized regression

[latexmath]
++++
D(p_1,\operatorname{sg}(\bar z_2))
=2-2\frac{p_1^\top\bar z_2}{\lVert p_1\rVert_2\lVert\bar z_2\rVert_2},
++++

where \(\operatorname{sg}\) denotes stop-gradient. A second term swaps the views. The online predictor is trained to match the target feature, while the target feature is treated as fixed for that gradient calculation.

The target parameters change according to

[latexmath]
++++
\bar\theta\leftarrow m\bar\theta+(1-m)\theta,\qquad
\bar\phi\leftarrow m\bar\phi+(1-m)\phi,
++++

with \(m\) close to one. The target is therefore a smoothed history of the online network, not an independently optimized network. This is the defining difference from ordinary shared-weight Siamese regression.


SimSiam (Chen and He, 2021) uses the same encoder and projector for both views and a predictor on each branch, but no EMA target network. With \(z_i=g(f(x_i))\) and \(p_i=q(z_i)\), its symmetric loss has the form

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{SimSiam}}=
D(p_1,\operatorname{sg}(z_2))
+D(p_2,\operatorname{sg}(z_1)),
++++

where \(D\) is negative cosine similarity. The stop-gradient leaves a target's forward value unchanged but removes the gradient path through that target in the corresponding term. Because the loss is symmetric, each shared branch acts as predictor in one term and target in the other. BYOL instead maintains a separate target network whose parameters follow the EMA update.

The predictor and stop-gradient change the optimization dynamics; they do not remove the constant-output solution from the mathematical objective. The original SimSiam study found that stop-gradient was essential to its proposed configuration. That is a method-specific result, not a theorem that any Siamese network with a detached branch must learn non-collapsed features.



[#unique_section2_ch3_011_dino_and_self_distillation_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== DINO: match a teacher's distribution

DINO (Caron et al., 2021) matches distributions rather than regressing one feature vector onto another. The student and teacher map different views to logits over learned prototype outputs. If \(a_s(x)\) and \(a_t(x)\) are their logits, the student and teacher distributions are

[latexmath]
++++
p_s(x)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{a_s(x)}{T_s}\right),\qquad
q_t(x)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{a_t(x)-c}{T_t}\right).
++++

Here \(c\) is a running center of teacher outputs, \(T_t\) controls teacher-target sharpness, and the teacher parameters follow an EMA of the student parameters. The cross-entropy between \(q_t\) and \(p_s\) is minimized across selected, different views. Centering discourages the teacher's outputs from concentrating persistently on a small subset of prototypes; a low teacher temperature sharpens the target distribution. These operations work together with the multi-crop training recipe.

Centering and sharpening have different roles: centering modifies output statistics to resist concentration on a small set of prototypes; sharpening lowers the entropy of the teacher's target distribution. Prototype indices are learned output coordinates, not automatically human-defined categories. DINO is best understood as self-distillation with a prototype distribution, rather than as an ordinary clustering algorithm.

Multi-crop training supplies a useful extra constraint. The teacher receives the larger global crops, while the student also sees smaller local crops. A local crop usually lacks some of the global context, so its output must match a target produced from a different field of view. This encourages some features to remain consistent across scales. It does not mean that every local crop contains enough evidence to infer every property of the entire scene; the target and crop protocol define what consistency is encouraged. The prototype outputs are learned coordinates, not labels assigned by a person, and their semantics must be established through evaluation.

DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2023) combines self-distillation with masked-patch prediction and a large-scale training recipe. DINOv3 (Siméoni et al., 2025) scales the family further and introduces Gram anchoring to reduce the degradation of dense feature maps during long training. These versions illustrate that data curation, architecture, optimization and feature diagnostics matter alongside the named loss. They are related training systems, not controlled comparisons that isolate one change.


[#unique_section2_ch3_012_redundancy_reduction_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Redundancy reduction and explicit variance constraints

BYOL, SimSiam and DINO make training dynamics or teacher targets central to avoiding trivial solutions. Barlow Twins (Zbontar et al., 2021) and VICReg (Bardes et al., 2022) express another idea: require paired views to agree, but constrain the statistics of features across a batch.


[#unique_section2_ch3_013_barlow_twins_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Barlow Twins: cross-correlation toward identity

Let latexmath:[Z^A] and latexmath:[Z^B] be feature batches from two views. Normalize each feature dimension across the batch and define cross-correlation latexmath:[C]:

[latexmath]
++++
C_{ij}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\tilde z^A_{b,i}\tilde z^B_{b,j}.
\tag{3.7}
++++

If corresponding dimensions agree and distinct dimensions are uncorrelated across views, latexmath:[C] approaches the identity matrix. Barlow Twins minimizes the diagonal mismatch and penalizes off-diagonal cross-correlation:

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{BT}}=\sum_i(1-C_{ii})^2+
\lambda\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}C_{ij}^{2}.
\tag{3.8}
++++

If (C_{ii}) is below one, the corresponding coordinate is not perfectly aligned across views. A nonzero (C_{ij}), for (i\ne j), means coordinate (i) in one view is linearly correlated with coordinate (j) in the other. The loss pushes the cross-correlation matrix toward identity. It controls second-order cross-view statistics; it does not establish statistical independence for arbitrary distributions, nor does it assign a meaningful physical interpretation to each coordinate.


[#unique_section2_ch3_014_vicreg_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== VICReg: make three constraints explicit

VICReg separates invariance, variance and covariance. For two feature batches latexmath:[Z] and latexmath:[Z'], the invariance term is a mean-squared difference:

[latexmath]
++++
s(Z,Z')=\frac{1}{N}\lVert Z-Z'\rVert_F^2.
\tag{3.9}
++++

For each feature dimension latexmath:[j], let latexmath:[\sigma_j(Z)] be its standard deviation over the batch. A variance penalty puts a floor under each dimension's standard deviation:

[latexmath]
++++
v(Z)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}
\left[\max\!\left(0,\gamma-\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Z_j)+\epsilon}\right)\right]^2.
\tag{3.10}
++++

The covariance term penalizes the squared off-diagonal entries of the empirical covariance matrix:

[latexmath]
++++
c(Z)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i\ne j}\operatorname{Cov}(Z)_{ij}^{2}.
\tag{3.11}
++++

The complete objective combines the three terms:

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{VICReg}}=\lambda s(Z,Z')
+\mu\,[v(Z)+v(Z')]
+\nu\,[c(Z)+c(Z')],
++++

where \(\lambda,\mu,\nu\) set their relative weights. The invariance term aligns paired views. The variance term penalizes a feature dimension whose standard deviation falls below \(\gamma\). The covariance term discourages linear redundancy between different dimensions. The variance hinge is squared in the original objective; that detail changes the gradient and should not be dropped when reproducing the formula.

If every example has the same value in one dimension, its batch standard deviation is zero and the variance penalty is positive for any positive margin. That makes the anti-collapse constraint explicit. It does not prove that the surviving variation is semantically relevant. A feature can vary with background texture and still satisfy a variance floor.

The methods are related but not identical. Barlow Twins uses normalized cross-correlation as the central matrix objective. VICReg uses a direct invariance term, a standard-deviation floor and a covariance penalty. Their shared lesson is that view agreement and non-collapse are separate requirements. Their moment constraints are not a guarantee of a world-model-ready state.

[#unique_section2_ch3_016_brief_other_methods_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Other methods in brief

**DeepCluster** alternates offline clustering of current features with training on the resulting pseudo-labels. **SwAV** forms prototype assignments online and predicts assignments across views, using a balancing step. These methods differ mainly in how their pseudo-targets are generated and refreshed.

**CLIP** aligns paired images and text with contrastive learning. **BERT** predicts masked tokens from bidirectional context; **GPT-style causal modeling** predicts the next token from preceding tokens. These examples show that self-supervised targets can use natural pairings or sequence structure, not just augmented images. Their details are outside this chapter's main derivations.


CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) aligns paired images and text with a contrastive objective; SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) replaces the batchwise softmax with pairwise sigmoid losses. Speech systems construct targets from audio itself: wav2vec 2.0 contrasts quantized latent targets, HuBERT predicts cluster-derived labels, and data2vec predicts contextualized teacher features. In language, BERT predicts selected masked tokens from bidirectional context, GPT-style models predict the next token from preceding context, and ELECTRA predicts whether tokens have been replaced. These methods differ in the target and available context; their full architectures are outside this chapter's main derivations.

[#unique_section2_ch3_022_predictive_latent_targets_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Predicting target representations

Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) predict target-encoder representations from a context representation instead of reconstructing every target pixel or identifying a target among explicit negatives. I-JEPA applies this idea to image regions; video variants extend context and target selection across time. The key design decision is which target regions are predicted from which context. Their detailed objectives belong in the next chapter. Here, they complete the comparison of target choices: agreement, candidate identification, feature statistics, pixels, discrete codes and contextualized representations all impose different constraints.

[#unique_section_ch3_016_masked_prediction_ID,reftext={chapter}.{counter:section},caption='{chapter}.{section}']
== Masked prediction: choose what the model must recover

[#unique_section2_ch3_017_one_mask_many_targets_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== One mask, several target spaces

Suppose an input is divided into visible positions latexmath:[V] and masked positions latexmath:[M]. An encoder processes the allowed context and a prediction head estimates a target latexmath:[y_i] at masked positions:

[latexmath]
++++
L=\frac{1}{|M|}\sum_{i\in M}\ell(\hat y_i,y_i).
\tag{3.12}
++++

This form leaves the crucial choice open: what is the target? It might be pixel values, a discrete code, a word token, a replaced-token label or a contextualized feature. The mask tells the model where information is withheld; the target tells it what relationship must be learned.

[#unique_section2_ch3_018_mae_pixels_and_asymmetry_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== MAE: predict masked pixels

A masked autoencoder (MAE; He et al., 2022) divides an image into patches and hides a large fraction of them. Only visible patches pass through the encoder. A smaller decoder receives the encoded visible patches and learned mask tokens inserted at the missing positions; it predicts pixel values at the original patch locations. The asymmetric design saves encoder computation because the encoder never processes the hidden tokens.

Let \(m_i=1\) when patch \(i\) is masked and \(0\) otherwise, with patch target \(x_i\) and prediction \(\hat x_i\). The reconstruction objective is

[latexmath]
++++
L_{\mathrm{MAE}}=
\frac{\sum_i m_i\,\lVert x_i-\hat x_i\rVert_2^2}
{\sum_i m_i}.
++++

The normalization is over masked patches, so visible patches do not contribute to the loss. Under squared error, the conditional mean is the best prediction in expected squared-error terms. If several patch contents are plausible from the visible context, the conditional mean can blur those alternatives. Pixel reconstruction can nevertheless learn useful features; its limitation is that pixel accuracy does not guarantee that the retained encoder feature preserves every variable needed for a later prediction. The encoder, rather than the reconstruction decoder, is usually the component transferred to a downstream task.

=== BEiT, iBOT and data2vec: change the target

BEiT (Bao et al., 2022) predicts discrete visual-token IDs produced by a learned tokenizer. Rather than regress pixel values, the model classifies the tokenizer's code for each masked patch. The tokenizer introduces its own prior: useful structure can be learned from the code vocabulary, but tokenizer errors and biases enter the target.

iBOT (Zhou et al., 2022) combines masked patch prediction with teacher-student self-distillation. It is an example where a family label is insufficient: the input is masked, target distributions come from a teacher, and prototype outputs structure those targets. Other masked-image methods deliberately change the target: SimMIM (Xie et al., 2022) uses a simple pixel-reconstruction objective; MaskFeat (Wei et al., 2022) predicts hand-designed visual features such as HOG rather than raw pixels; BEiT v2 improves the discrete-target route with semantic visual tokenization. These variations reinforce the same principle: the mask alone does not define the learning task.

data2vec (Baevski et al., 2022) predicts contextualized teacher features from masked input and was designed as a framework spanning speech, vision and language. A latent target may contain broader context than a local pixel target, but matching a teacher does not automatically ensure that the feature preserves all future-relevant or causal state information. In every case, the representation is shaped by the selected target.

BERT-style masked language modeling predicts selected corrupted tokens from bidirectional context. GPT-style causal language modeling predicts the next token from preceding tokens only. ELECTRA trains a discriminator to detect replaced tokens. These are brief analogies for target/context construction, not additional implementation tutorials.

[#unique_section_ch3_024_latent_bottlenecks_ID,reftext={chapter}.{counter:section},caption='{chapter}.{section}']
== Latent bottlenecks

[#unique_section2_ch3_024_bottleneck_dimension_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Dimension is capacity, not useful information

A latent bottleneck maps a high-dimensional observation or token grid into a constrained representation. The simplest form is a vector \(h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\). Lowering \(d\) limits the number of coordinates, but dimension alone does not measure useful information. A small vector may preserve the few variables needed for a prediction; a wider vector may devote many dimensions to texture, illumination or other nuisance variation.

The information-bottleneck principle expresses this trade-off using mutual information. Let \(X\) be the observation, \(Z\) its representation and \(Y\) a target relevant to the later task. A conceptual objective is

[latexmath]
++++
\min \; I(X;Z)-\beta I(Z;Y).
++++

The first term penalizes information about the input retained by \(Z\); the second rewards information about \(Y\). The coefficient \(\beta\) controls the trade-off. This is a theoretical lens, not the loss explicitly optimized by every method in this chapter. Mutual information for deterministic continuous representations requires care; practical SSL objectives usually optimize a tractable proxy.

The important question is not “how small can the vector be?” It is “which distinctions can be discarded without changing the predictions we need to make?” A representation useful for image retrieval may be insufficient for predicting motion if it merges observations with different velocities or hidden states.

[#unique_section2_ch3_025_projection_head_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{counter:subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== The projection head and the feature that survives

Many contrastive and bootstrap methods use a projection head between an encoder and the objective:

[latexmath]
++++
h=f_\theta(x),\qquad z=g_\phi(h).
\tag{3.13}
++++

The loss is calculated on latexmath:[z], while the representation evaluated downstream is often latexmath:[h]. A projection head gives the pretraining objective a dedicated space in which to impose its constraints. It does not follow that latexmath:[z] and latexmath:[h] have the same geometry or serve the same downstream purpose.

The exported tensor must be identified explicitly. Some evaluations use the feature (h) before the projector; others use intermediate or patch-level transformer features. A low pretraining loss measured on (z) is not itself evidence that the feature (h) has the same geometry or usefulness.


[#unique_section2_ch3_026_global_local_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== A global vector or a grid of local features?

A global vector is compact and convenient for classification, retrieval and simple interfaces. A grid of patch features preserves approximate locations and correspondences. A temporal sequence preserves history only if its aggregation does not erase the changes needed by prediction. These forms are related but not interchangeable.

Global pooling can intentionally discard spatial layout. That may be appropriate for image-level classification or retrieval but harmful when predictions depend on location or correspondence between regions. A patch grid retains more spatial structure at the cost of memory and downstream computation. A temporal state can incorporate history, but the representation must retain motion-related information when it changes the future distribution.


[#unique_section2_ch3_027_compression_sufficiency_ID,reftext={chapter}.{section}.{subsection},caption='{chapter}.{section}.{subsection}']
=== Compression and predictive sufficiency

A useful representation retains enough information for the relevant prediction while suppressing nuisance variation. There is no universally optimal latent dimension for all tasks. In some settings a small state may be sufficient; in others, spatial features, a history window, proprioception or multiple latent components may be necessary.

Here, *compact* means constrained and usable by downstream dynamics or planning, not necessarily information-theoretically minimal. The bottleneck should be selected and tested against the downstream task. A low-dimensional representation that aliases important states is not a success; a larger representation is not automatically better if it mostly stores nuisance detail.

[#unique_section_ch3_026_a_world_model_audit_ID,reftext={chapter}.{counter:section},caption='{chapter}.{section}']
== Compact state representations

A low self-supervised loss establishes only that the representation satisfies its training objective. A linear probe, \(\hat y=Wz+b\), tests whether selected variables can be recovered by a linear map from \(z\). A poor linear-probe score does not prove that the information is absent: it may be encoded nonlinearly. A high score shows that the tested variables are accessible to that probe, but not that the representation contains every distinction needed for future prediction.

Evaluation must match the claim. Feature variance and covariance can expose some collapse modes. Held-out probes test the accessibility of chosen variables. Reconstruction measures agreement with the reconstruction target. Future-latent retrieval measures identification among the sampled candidates. None of these scores alone establishes predictive sufficiency.

A representation \(Z\) is sufficient for a target future \(Y\), relative to the available observation \(X\), when

[latexmath]
++++
P(Y\mid X)=P(Y\mid Z).
++++

This equality says that once \(Z\) is known, the original observation provides no further information about \(Y\). If two observations map to the same \(z\) but imply different future distributions, the representation has aliased a distinction relevant to that prediction. That is the failure a useful state must avoid.

The important tests are distinct: inspect the transformations and target that define the pretraining problem; examine whether the features collapse; probe variables on held-out data; and evaluate prediction of the quantity for which the representation will be used.

[#unique_section_ch3_027_summary_ID,reftext={chapter}.{counter:section},caption='{chapter}.{section}']
== Summary

Self-supervised learning constructs a training target from data. Contrastive methods identify a positive among sampled candidates; their gradients depend on the candidate distribution, and their information-theoretic interpretation requires specific sampling assumptions. BYOL and SimSiam predict a detached feature target but differ in whether that target is updated by EMA. DINO matches teacher output distributions. Barlow Twins penalizes departures of cross-view correlation from identity, while VICReg separates view agreement, a variance floor and covariance regularization.

Masked prediction changes both the hidden context and the target: MAE predicts pixels, BEiT predicts discrete codes, and iBOT/data2vec use teacher-derived targets. The choice of target determines what errors are penalized. The latent bottleneck then determines which features are retained and which distinctions may be lost. A useful representation must preserve enough information for the prediction of interest; the pretraining loss by itself cannot establish that condition.
