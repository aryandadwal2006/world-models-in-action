# 4.2.5 LeJEPA and Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization

LeJEPA, introduced by Balestriero and LeCun (2025), combines a predictive multi-view objective with a distributional regularizer called Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization (SIGReg). Its central proposal is that, under the downstream prediction-risk framework studied in the paper, an isotropic Gaussian embedding distribution is optimal. SIGReg then provides a practical way to encourage that distribution without evaluating a high-dimensional density directly.

The theorem and the implementation answer different questions. The theory studies what embedding distribution minimizes a specified downstream risk. SIGReg is a stochastic numerical objective that encourages the learned embeddings to approach that target. The latter does not turn a finite minibatch into a certificate that the population embedding distribution is exactly Gaussian.

## The Gaussian target and the scope of the theory

Let an encoder produce an embedding (Z=f_\theta(X)\in\mathbb R^d). The paper studies downstream prediction from frozen representations. In its linear setting, a probe fits a regularized least-squares estimator. The analysis compares embedding geometries under matched representational capacity, including a comparison between isotropic and anisotropic covariance structures. Anisotropy can increase the worst-case bias of a regularized linear estimator and the variance of an unregularized estimator.

The paper also analyzes nonlinear probes, including radius-based nearest-neighbour regression and kernel regression. Under the regularity conditions and fixed-variance constraints stated in the paper, the analysis identifies the isotropic Gaussian as the unique minimizing distribution for the risk terms considered. The Gaussian's score function and the associated Fisher-information functional are central to the nonlinear analysis.

This is a meaningful theoretical result, but its scope must remain explicit. It concerns the downstream estimator families, risk criterion, regularity assumptions and fixed-scale constraints used in the paper. It is not a theorem that Gaussian latents are optimal for every conceivable task, every control problem, every conditional transition model or every data distribution. A task may need discrete modes, constraints, multimodality or structured variables that a simple isotropic Gaussian prior does not express on its own.

The useful design principle is therefore conditional: if the goal is to support the class of downstream estimators and risk assumptions analyzed in LeJEPA, isotropic Gaussian embeddings provide the paper's optimal target. The chapter should not turn that statement into an unrestricted claim about semantic quality or physical sufficiency.

## From a multivariate test to one-dimensional projections

Suppose the empirical embedding distribution is (P_\theta) and the desired target is (Q=\mathcal N(0,I_d)). Directly testing whether a high-dimensional sample comes from (Q) can be computationally expensive. SIGReg instead projects the embeddings onto unit directions.

For a unit vector (a\in\mathbb S^{d-1}), the scalar projection is
[
U=a^\top Z.
]
If (Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)), then (U\sim\mathcal N(0,1)) for every unit direction (a). A directional test can therefore compare the projected sample with a univariate standard-normal target.

The Cramér-Wold theorem supplies the identification principle: a multivariate probability distribution is uniquely determined by the family of all its one-dimensional projected distributions. If every one-dimensional projection of (P) matches the corresponding projection of (Q), the two distributions are equal. That is an all-directions statement.

SIGReg uses a finite collection of directions at each training step. It resamples directions during training, so the optimizer sees a changing set of one-dimensional views over time. This stochastic sketch is computationally practical, but a finite minibatch and a finite number of directions do not prove exact equality of the population distributions. The paper's analysis also studies how embedding smoothness and repeated random directions affect how strongly a finite sketch constrains the full distribution.

## Characteristic functions

For a scalar random variable (U), its characteristic function is
[
\varphi_U(t)=\mathbb E[e^{itU}].
]
For a standard normal random variable,
[
\varphi_{\mathcal N(0,1)}(t)=e^{-t^2/2}.
]
Given projected samples (u_1,\ldots,u_B), the empirical characteristic function is
[
\widehat\varphi_B(t)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}e^{itu_b}.
]

The empirical characteristic function is a finite average of complex exponentials. It is differentiable with respect to the samples and can be aggregated across distributed workers using sum-reduction operations. Unlike high-order moments, the complex exponentials remain bounded as the magnitude of the input grows.

## The Epps-Pulley statistic

LeJEPA uses an Epps-Pulley-style statistic to compare an empirical characteristic function with the target characteristic function:
[
T_{EP}(u_{1:B})
=
B\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\left|\widehat\varphi_B(t)-\varphi_0(t)\right|^2
w(t)\,dt,
]
where \(\varphi_0(t)=e^{-t^2/2}\) and \(w(t)\) is a weighting function, typically Gaussian. The weighting controls how discrepancies across frequencies contribute to the statistic.

This formula is the population-integral description. A practical implementation approximates the integral by numerical quadrature over a finite set of frequencies. The LeJEPA paper reports a trapezoidal approximation using a small number of integration knots, and its experiments ablate the range and number of points. It also discusses the finite-minibatch bias in the empirical statistic, which vanishes at an \(\mathcal O(1/B)\) rate in the paper's analysis.

SIGReg samples directions \(a_1,\ldots,a_M\), computes the projected samples \(a_m^\top z_b\), evaluates a univariate statistic for each direction, and aggregates the directional losses. For the training objective, the paper averages directional statistics rather than taking their maximum. The maximum appears in the testing-theoretic construction; averaging is chosen in the optimization objective to avoid sparse gradients concentrated on only the worst direction.

A schematic training loss is
[
\mathcal L_{SIGReg}(Z)
=
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}
T_{EP}\big(a_m^\top z_1,\ldots,a_m^\top z_B\big).
]
The precise quadrature range, frequency weights, direction sampling, normalization and distributed aggregation are implementation details and should be read from the cited paper version and official code, not inferred from this schematic.

## The LeJEPA objective

LeJEPA uses multiple global and local views of an observation. Let (z_{n,v}) denote the embedding for sample (n) and view (v). Let the first (V_g) views be global, and let (V=V_g+V_l) include all global and local views. The paper's prediction term encourages each view to agree with the mean embedding of the global views:
[
\mu_n=\frac{1}{V_g}\sum_{v=1}^{V_g}z_{n,v},
\qquad
\mathcal L_{pred}
=
\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}
\|\mu_n-z_{n,v}\|_2^2,
]
averaged across samples. SIGReg is applied to the embedding sets for the views. The total objective is
[
\mathcal L_{LeJEPA}
=
(1-\lambda)\mathcal L_{pred}
+
\lambda\mathcal L_{SIGReg}.
]

This formulation differs from the common EMA teacher-predictor JEPA. In the specified LeJEPA recipe, the model does not require an EMA teacher, stop-gradient target branch or a predictor network. It uses a prediction term over multiple views and an explicit distributional regularizer. It should not be generalized into a claim that no JEPA requires target stabilization or that every target-prediction architecture can remove its predictor without changing the task.

## Why characteristic functions?

The paper compares moment-based, cumulative-distribution and characteristic-function tests. Moment tests can be sensitive to outliers and require higher moments to distinguish more distributional structure. CDF-based tests often involve sorting or order statistics, creating synchronization and differentiability difficulties in distributed gradient training. The characteristic function is differentiable and naturally amenable to averaging across data-parallel workers.

The numerical quadrature is an approximation. The Epps-Pulley statistic is the statistical object; the particular grid, frequency range and finite batch define its practical estimate. LeJEPA reports linear memory and computational complexity in minibatch size for its implementation, while the full cost also depends on embedding dimension, number of projected directions and quadrature points. Complexity statements must state which quantities are held fixed.

## What SIGReg does and does not establish

SIGReg provides a differentiable distributional signal that goes beyond mean and covariance matching. It samples one-dimensional projections and compares their empirical characteristic functions with a normal target. The repeated sampling of directions is central to its sketching interpretation.

It does not establish exact Gaussianity from one training step. It does not guarantee that the representation is a sufficient statistic for every environment. It does not ensure calibrated uncertainty or action-conditioned dynamics. It does not prove that distances in latent space correspond to physical distances. And the theorem's downstream optimum should not be read outside its assumptions.

The LeJEPA paper reports stability across different architectures, datasets and hyperparameters, including scaling to very large models. It also reports that training loss correlates with linear-probe performance in the tested ImageNet setup. These are empirical findings of that paper, not a universal guarantee that training loss always replaces downstream evaluation.
