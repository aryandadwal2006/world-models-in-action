# Mathematical foundations for reading SSL papers

This is a self-contained notation and statistics reference for the methods in this dossier. It derives the mathematical tools repeatedly used in SSL so later equations do not rely on unnamed intuition.

## 1. Random variables, samples and distributions

A random variable X follows a probability distribution \(p_X(x)\). A dataset \(x_1,\ldots,x_N\) is a finite sample drawn from some distribution. The empirical distribution is

\[
\hat p_N(x)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_i}(x),
\]

where \(\delta_{x_i}\) is a point mass. Training optimizes expectations under a data distribution, while code approximates them using minibatch averages. Batch size, shuffling, duplicates, class imbalance and distributed-sampling rules determine how accurate that approximation is.

A joint distribution \(p(x,y)\) specifies how X and Y occur together. Marginals are \(p(x)\) and \(p(y)\); conditional probability is \(p(y\mid x)=p(x,y)/p(x)\) when \(p(x)>0\). Independence means \(p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)\): observing X does not change the distribution of Y.

SSL target construction is a choice of distributions. Positive-pair samplers create a joint-like relationship; contrastive negatives often approximate independent marginal samples; masked prediction defines a conditional target given visible context; teacher methods compare a student output with a target distribution generated by another model.

## 2. Entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence

For a discrete random variable X, entropy is

\[
H(X)=-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x).
\]

For a target distribution q and model distribution p, cross-entropy is

\[
H(q,p)=-\sum_k q_k\log p_k.
\]

If q is one-hot at class y, this reduces to \(-\log p_y\). Cross-entropy penalizes assigning low probability to the correct target.

The Kullback–Leibler divergence is

\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)=\sum_k q_k\log\frac{q_k}{p_k}.
\]

It is nonnegative, with equality when the distributions match under standard conditions. Since \(H(q,p)=H(q)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)\), minimizing cross-entropy to a fixed q is equivalent to minimizing KL from q to p. Direction matters: KL is generally asymmetric.

### Softmax

Given logits \(a_1,\ldots,a_K\),

\[
p_k=\frac{e^{a_k}}{\sum_j e^{a_j}}.
\]

The exponential gives positive weights; the denominator normalizes them. Adding a constant to every logit leaves p unchanged. Temperature modifies concentration:

\[
p_k(\tau)=\frac{e^{a_k/\tau}}{\sum_j e^{a_j/\tau}}.
\]

For one-hot target y,

\[
L=-\log p_y=-a_y+\log\sum_j e^{a_j},
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_k}=p_k-\mathbf1[k=y].
\]

This is the common local derivative for contrastive candidate classification and discrete token/prototype prediction.

## 3. Dot products, cosine similarity and normalization

For vectors \(u,v\in\mathbb R^d\), the dot product \(u^\top v\) combines direction and magnitude. Cosine similarity normalizes out magnitude:

\[
\cos(u,v)=\frac{u^\top v}{\|u\|_2\|v\|_2}.
\]

For unit-norm vectors, cosine similarity equals the dot product and lies in [-1,1]. Normalization changes the optimization: gradients through the normalized vector are projected onto the tangent space orthogonal to its direction, rather than freely changing norm and direction.

This is why a paper must specify whether similarities use raw vectors, L2-normalized vectors, projector outputs or backbone features. These choices change the feature geometry and gradient.

## 4. Mean, variance, covariance and correlation

For batch features \(Z\in\mathbb R^{N\times d}\), with each row a sample, the mean is

\[
\mu=\frac1N\sum_{b=1}^{N}z_b.
\]

Center the samples as \(Z_c=Z-\mathbf1\mu^\top\). Sample covariance is commonly

\[
C=\frac1{N-1}Z_c^\top Z_c.
\]

Diagonal entries record per-dimension variance. Off-diagonal \(C_{ij}\) records how dimensions i and j vary together linearly. Correlation divides covariance by standard deviations, making each coordinate scale-free when standard deviations are nonzero.

Diagonal covariance does not imply statistical independence without additional assumptions, such as joint Gaussianity. The identity covariance \(C=I\) says features are centered, unit variance and pairwise uncorrelated in the empirical second-order sense. It does not imply semantic factors, invertibility, causal variables or Markov sufficiency.

### Whitening

For positive-definite covariance \(C=U\Lambda U^\top\), a whitening map applies approximately \(C^{-1/2}=U\Lambda^{-1/2}U^\top\) to centered features. Its output covariance is

\[
C^{-1/2}CC^{-1/2}=I.
\]

The inverse square root amplifies small-eigenvalue directions, so noisy covariance estimates and near-singular matrices can make whitening unstable. Implementations often add \(\epsilon I\), clip eigenvalues or use other regularized approximations. These details are part of the method.

## 5. Rank, singular values and dimensional collapse

Let \(Z_c\in\mathbb R^{N\times d}\) be a centered feature matrix with singular value decomposition

\[
Z_c=U\Sigma V^\top.
\]

Singular values \(\sigma_1\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_r>0\) describe variation along orthogonal directions. Numerical rank counts singular values above a declared tolerance. Effective rank summarizes the spectrum using an entropy-like statistic.

A constant feature dimension has zero variance, but dimensional collapse can happen even when every coordinate has some variance: coordinates may be highly correlated so most energy lies in a low-dimensional subspace. Variance floors and rank diagnostics answer different questions.

A common normalized spectrum is \(p_i=\sigma_i/\sum_j\sigma_j\), with entropy \(H(p)=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i\) and effective rank \(\exp(H(p))\). Other papers use squared singular values or covariance eigenvalues. Never report “effective rank” without stating the convention.

## 6. Mutual information

Mutual information is

\[
I(X;Y)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(x,y)\|p(x)p(y))
=\mathbb E_{p(x,y)}\left[\log\frac{p(y\mid x)}{p(y)}\right].
\]

It is zero under independence and positive when observing X changes the distribution of Y. It measures statistical dependence, not semantic usefulness or causality.

A deterministic representation \(Z=f(X)\) cannot create new information about Y that was absent from X. The data-processing inequality states that for a Markov chain \(X\to Z\to Y\), \(I(X;Y)\geq I(Z;Y)\). A representation can still make useful information easier to access with a restricted downstream model by changing geometry or discarding nuisance variation.

The information-bottleneck objective is often written

\[
L_{\mathrm{IB}}=I(Z;X)-\beta I(Z;Y).
\]

It trades compression of X against retention of information about Y. In SSL, Y is often not the downstream target but a proxy constructed from views, masks, pairs or teacher features. Maximizing information about a proxy can preserve nuisance dependence if the proxy is misaligned with the task.

## 7. Characteristic functions and Gaussian targets

For a scalar random variable X, the characteristic function is

\[
\varphi_X(t)=\mathbb E[e^{itX}].
\]

It is the Fourier transform of the probability distribution and uniquely determines that distribution. For \(X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\),

\[
\varphi_X(t)=e^{-t^2/2}.
\]

Given projected scalar samples \(u_1,\ldots,u_N\), the empirical characteristic function is \(\hat\varphi(t)=N^{-1}\sum_i e^{itu_i}\). A normality statistic measures discrepancy from \(e^{-t^2/2}\) over a prescribed set or weighted range of t values.

The Cramér–Wold theorem says a multivariate distribution is uniquely determined by its one-dimensional projections over all directions. Sketching methods draw a finite set of random directions to approximate that condition. A finite set of projections is a computational estimate, not exact proof that all high-dimensional distributions match.

## 8. Optimal transport and Wasserstein distance

A coupling \(\pi(x,y)\) specifies how mass from distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) is paired. The p-Wasserstein distance is

\[
W_p(\mu,\nu)=
\left[
\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}
\mathbb E_{(X,Y)\sim\pi}\|X-Y\|^p
\right]^{1/p},
\]

where \(\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) is the set of couplings with marginals \(\mu,\nu\). It measures the minimum expected transport cost between distributions.

In one dimension, the optimal coupling matches quantiles. For empirical samples, sort both sample sets and compare corresponding order statistics. Sliced Wasserstein averages such one-dimensional costs over projected directions.

This is the mathematical basis for VISReg's shape loss. Its projection count is a computational parameter. Matching finitely many slices approximates distribution shape but does not prove exact equality in every direction.

## 9. Exponential moving averages

Given online parameters \(\theta_t\), a target may update as

\[
\xi_t=m\xi_{t-1}+(1-m)\theta_t.
\]

Repeated substitution gives

\[
\xi_t=(1-m)\sum_{r=0}^{t-1}m^r\theta_{t-r}+m^t\xi_0.
\]

The target is an exponentially weighted history. A high m creates a smoother but laggier target. This is a temporal filter, not a variance floor or covariance constraint. No anti-collapse guarantee follows from the EMA equation alone.

## 10. Stop-gradient and detached targets

Let sg(z) mean that the forward value is z while its gradient is set to zero through that branch. In automatic differentiation this deletes selected terms from the chain rule. It does not mean the forward target stays constant for all future training: target values may change in later forward passes because their parameters are updated elsewhere.

Common cases:
- BYOL: target network is detached for the loss and its parameters are updated by EMA.
- SimSiam: the target branch is detached for each loss term, while shared parameters receive gradients through the prediction side.
- DINO: teacher probabilities are detached and teacher parameters update through EMA.
- VISReg: stop-gradient is applied to the scale in normalization for a different reason, namely separating shape regularization from scale updates.

“Stop-gradient” names an automatic-differentiation operation, not a complete explanation of an algorithm.

## 11. Conditional expectation and squared error

For context C and target Y, consider \(R(a\mid c)=\mathbb E[\|Y-a\|^2\mid C=c]\). With \(m=\mathbb E[Y\mid C=c]\),

\[
R(a\mid c)=\mathbb E[\|Y-m\|^2\mid c]+\|m-a\|^2.
\]

The cross term vanishes, so the minimum is attained at the conditional mean. If multiple targets are plausible, that mean can average between them. This matters when interpreting pixel reconstruction and latent regression, but it does not mean every reconstruction is blurry or every MSE-trained representation is useless.

## 12. Conditional independence and Markov sufficiency

The statement \((Y\perp H)\mid Z\) means that once Z is known, observing history H provides no additional information about target Y. In a controlled system, a useful predictive-sufficiency condition is

\[
P(O_{t+1},R_t\mid H_t,A_t)
=P(O_{t+1},R_t\mid Z_t,A_t).
\]

A static augmentation-consistency loss does not directly constrain this equality. It may learn features correlated with state variables but still merge histories with different conditional futures. Reconstruction or classification accuracy cannot prove this conditional-independence property for every action.

## 13. Notation and implementation conventions to record

Whenever a paper defines a loss, record:
- whether B means source images, views, tokens, frames or all candidates;
- whether the mean is over examples, masked elements, feature dimensions or views;
- whether covariance uses denominator B or B−1;
- whether feature vectors are normalized before similarity or after projection;
- whether the target branch is detached;
- whether batch statistics are local to one device or globally synchronized;
- whether a sum includes every pair or only off-diagonal entries once;
- whether a distance is squared, averaged or square-rooted;
- whether K means negative candidates, total candidates, prototype count or random projection directions.

A missing convention can change a gradient, scaling behavior or comparison. These are scientific choices, not cosmetic notation.
