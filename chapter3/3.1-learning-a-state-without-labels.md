# 3.1 Learning a state without labels

A useful state is not simply a smaller copy of an image. It is a description that preserves the information needed to understand the situation. For a moving system, that may include positions and velocities, even when those quantities are not directly available as labels.

Let $x$ denote an observation, such as an image rendered by a simulator. An encoder $f_\theta$, parameterized by $\theta$, transforms it into a representation $z$:

$$
\boxed{z=f_\theta(x)}
\tag{3.1}
$$

We will use $z$ for the representation throughout this chapter. It is a vector of $d$ numbers, where $d$ is the latent dimension. The encoder's job is to turn a high-dimensional observation into a form that later components of the world model can use.

But what should those numbers preserve? Chapter 2 gave us the answer at a conceptual level. A useful state must retain information that matters for predicting the future and discard information that does not. We can make that requirement concrete by separating two properties.

Sufficiency means that the representation preserves the information about the physical state that is available in the observation. If the representation loses a distinction that matters, a downstream dynamics model cannot recover it just by training harder.

Minimality means that the representation avoids carrying unnecessary information. For a cart-pole, this might mean retaining the cart's position and the pole's angle while ignoring irrelevant differences in brightness or background texture.

The two requirements must be considered together. An encoder that retains every pixel may preserve useful information, but it has not necessarily learned a compact state. An encoder that discards almost everything may be compact, but it can also lose the information needed to distinguish different situations.

#### Aliasing: when compression loses the wrong information

Suppose two cart-pole observations look identical. In one underlying situation, the cart is moving to the left; in the other, it is moving to the right. If both observations map to the same representation $z$, a downstream model receiving only that representation cannot distinguish the two situations.

This is an example of aliasing: different underlying states become indistinguishable in the representation.

#### Why not train directly on the state?

Let $y$ denote the physical quantities we want the representation to retain. In our experiments, $y$ consists of the simulator's generalized positions followed by its generalized velocities.

If $y$ were available for every training observation, we could train an encoder to predict these quantities directly. The prediction error would provide a clear signal about which information the encoder needs to retain. In real settings, however, the exact physical state is often unavailable.

There are three difficulties with relying on labelled data alone.

Cost. Human annotations and physical measurements require effort or additional instrumentation. An agent can collect images continuously, but obtaining an exact label for every image may be expensive.

Task dependence. Labels reflect the task for which they were designed. An encoder trained to recognize objects might learn their identities while neglecting motion information needed by a dynamics model.

Availability. A deployed agent does not automatically know the exact positions and velocities of every object it observes. It receives images and whatever additional sensor readings are available to it.

Labels remain useful whenever they can be obtained. A simulator can supply its internal state, a synthetic data generator can produce targets, and demonstrations can provide training signals. These are legitimate alternatives, each with its own requirements. Self-supervised learning addresses a different opportunity: the observations themselves contain relationships that can be turned into learning signals without manually labelling every example.

> **DEFINITION — Self-supervised learning**

Self-supervised learning trains a model using a target constructed from the data itself, rather than a human-provided label. The model solves a task designed to produce this target, often called a pretext task. The pretext task is a means of learning a representation; its answer is not necessarily the final goal.

Consider a simple example. Take an image, rotate it by a known amount, and ask a model to identify the rotation. We do not need a person to annotate the transformed image: we already know which rotation we applied. The transformation generates the target automatically.

This clarifies the distinction between three learning settings. In supervised learning, an externally supplied label tells the model what to predict. In classical unsupervised learning, an objective seeks structure in the inputs without using an externally supplied label or a separately constructed prediction target. In self-supervised learning, the model has a target, but the data or a transformation of the data supplies it.

The terminology varies across the literature. Some surveys treat self-supervised learning as a subset of unsupervised learning; other authors emphasize that self-supervised methods do use a supervisory signal, even though it is generated from the data. This chapter uses the more explicit distinction above. For a broader taxonomy of self-supervised methods, see Gui and colleagues' A Survey on Self-supervised Learning: Algorithms, Applications, and Future Trends (2024).

> **NOTE — Predicting what is missing**

In his ICLR 2020 keynote, Yann LeCun described self-supervision through the general idea of predicting missing information (LeCun, 2020). The missing part might be a region of an image, the future given the past, or an obscured portion of an observation. These are not the same objective, but each turns structure already present in the data into a training signal.

This idea provides a useful map of the chapter: first learn from transformations and views, then from missing visual content, and finally from temporal relationships.

#### The objective determines what the encoder is rewarded for keeping

Self-supervised learning is not one algorithm. It is a family of methods that construct different training signals. Each signal rewards the encoder for retaining some information, while leaving other information optional.

Rotation prediction asks the model to identify a transformation applied to an image. It rewards features useful for recognizing that transformation. Those features need not be the features most useful for predicting a system's future.

Contrastive learning creates two altered views of the same observation and trains their representations to identify one another. The choice of alterations matters: features that survive those transformations can help identify a positive pair, while features that vary may be treated as less useful.

Masked prediction hides part of an observation and asks the model to predict the missing content from what remains visible. It rewards information that helps reconstruction. Some of that information may describe physical state; some may describe visual details irrelevant to control.

Temporal prediction asks the model to use earlier observations to identify or predict later ones. It provides a learning signal tied to what happens over time, although success still depends on the data, the prediction task, and the model.

The central principle is therefore:

> **> **SIDEBAR — The principle behind this chapter****

A representation-learning objective specifies what information the representation is rewarded for retaining. Information the objective does not require may be discarded.

Contrastive learning rewards information that helps distinguish a positive view from other views. Masked prediction rewards information that helps reconstruct hidden content. Temporal prediction rewards information that helps identify or anticipate what comes next.

The objective is not the only influence. Architecture, training data, transformations, and training budget also shape the representation. A decreasing training loss does not, by itself, establish that the learned state is useful for prediction or planning.

This principle gives us a way to evaluate methods rather than simply collect them. We can examine what their objectives encourage, then measure what their learned representations actually retain.

#### Why use a benchmark rather than a toy world?

The experiments in this chapter use the DeepMind Control Suite (DMC), a collection of physics-based control tasks built on MuJoCo. The observations are rendered by the simulator rather than drawn by a custom world designed solely to demonstrate a particular learning method. The suite gives us both rendered images for training and simulator variables against which to evaluate the resulting representations.

Our experiments use three tasks, shown in Figure 3.2.

- **Cartpole balance.** A pole is hinged to a cart that moves along a track. Its evaluation state has four variables: two generalized positions and two generalized velocities.

- **Finger spin.** A planar, two-joint finger interacts with a free body that can rotate. Its evaluation state has six variables: three generalized positions and three generalized velocities.

- **Cheetah run.** A planar, legged body moves across a surface. Its evaluation state has eighteen variables: nine generalized positions and nine generalized velocities.

Cartpole balance is the main task because its small state makes results relatively easy to interpret. Finger spin provides a second task for the objective comparison in Section 3.5. Cheetah run is used in Section 3.6 to examine how the size of the representation affects a task with a higher-dimensional state.

#### The experimental contract

The data-collection code records rendered frames and the corresponding simulator state. It samples actions uniformly within each task's action bounds, steps the environment, and caches the observations and state variables. The training and validation datasets are collected using independent environment seeds.

The key distinction is between training signal and evaluation target. For the self-supervised experiments, the training objectives receive rendered frames, not the stored physics-state labels. We use those labels to evaluate which physical quantities can be recovered from a learned representation. Section 3.5 also includes a separate supervised reference that is trained using state labels; that model is explicitly treated as a supervised comparison, not as a self-supervised method.

> **NOTE — Why the simulator state is still useful**

Using state variables for evaluation does not make the self-supervised objective supervised. It gives us a reference against which to test the representation after learning. The distinction must remain explicit: the self-supervised encoder does not receive these labels as its training target, while the supervised reference later in the chapter does.
