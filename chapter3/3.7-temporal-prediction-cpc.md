# 3.7 Predicting over time: from invariance to prediction

### 3.7.1 Why the future is not just another view

Return to the view-generation decision from Section 3.3. Two augmented versions of the same frame are positive examples because the objective asks the encoder to preserve information that survives those augmentations. The brightness and small crop changes are intended to be nuisance variations, so the learned representation should be relatively insensitive to them.

Now suppose we instead pair frames from times $t$ and $t+\Delta$ as positives and ask their embeddings to agree. The scene may have changed during that interval. The cart may have moved, the pole may have rotated, and the object's velocity may have changed. An objective that rewards agreement between the two embeddings can treat these differences as information to suppress.

That may be undesirable for a world model. The future depends on the current situation and its dynamics. If the representation is encouraged to remove the differences that signal those dynamics, a downstream predictor has less useful information to work with.

This is not a claim that temporal contrastive learning must always discard dynamics. The result depends on how temporal positives are constructed and what else the objective asks the model to preserve. The narrower point is that invariance and prediction impose different requirements. Invariance says which observations should agree; prediction says which future observations are compatible with a given past.

Our measurements from the earlier sections make this distinction concrete. Table 3.3 showed that velocity was poorly decoded from the learned single-frame representation under the tested configuration. Stacking two frames did not reliably fix the problem under those same experimental conditions. These results do not prove that velocity is fundamentally absent from images or impossible to recover from short sequences. They show that the representation and training setup did not make it reliably linearly available.

A frame-level invariance objective has no direct reason to preserve velocity. A temporal objective can instead reward a context for retaining information that helps identify a future observation. CPC is one way to implement that change.

### 3.7.2 Contrastive predictive coding

CPC, introduced by van den Oord and colleagues, learns by predicting future representations rather than reconstructing future pixels. It encodes a sequence of observations into latent vectors, summarizes the available past in a context vector, and trains that context to identify the correct future latent among other candidates.

Let a sequence of observations be $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_T$. A shared encoder $f_\theta$ maps each observation to a latent representation:

$$
z_t=f_\theta(x_t).
$$

A recurrent model summarizes the representations observed up to time $t$:

$$
c_t=g(z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_t).
$$

The context $c_t$ may depend on the current representation and every earlier representation in the sequence. It must not depend on future representations $z_{t+1},z_{t+2},\ldots$. Otherwise, the task would leak information from the answer into the context used to select that answer.

We use a gated recurrent unit $GRU$ for $g$. A GRU maintains a learned hidden state as it processes the sequence one step at a time. Its purpose here is to produce a compact context vector, not to guarantee that every relevant aspect of the past is retained. Whether the learned context retains useful information is an empirical question.

#### Scoring a candidate future

For each prediction horizon $k$, CPC learns a matrix $W_k$. It scores a candidate future latent using a bilinear compatibility function:

$$
\exp\!\left(z_{t+k}^{\top}W_kc_t\right).
\tag{3.20}
$$

Here, $z_{t+k}$ is the candidate representation at a future time, $c_t$ is the context available at time $t$, and $W_k$ is specific to horizon $k$. The matrix lets the model learn which relationships between context coordinates and future latent coordinates are useful for identifying a match.

The exponent turns the raw bilinear score into a positive compatibility value. As in equation 3.5, the objective normalizes these values across candidates to obtain a probability distribution. Higher compatibility means that a candidate receives a larger share of the probability assigned by the context.

Consider a batch containing $B$ sequences. For a context from sequence $i$, the correct target is the future latent from sequence $i$. Future latents from the other sequences in the batch act as negative candidates. At horizon $k$, define the raw score

$$
\left(z_{t+k}^{(j)}\right)^\top
W_kc_t^{(i)},
$$

where $i$ identifies the context and $j$ identifies a candidate future. The probability assigned to the correct candidate is

$$
\frac{\exp(s_{ii}^{(k)})}
{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{B}\exp(s_{ij}^{(k)})}.
$$

The loss for that context and horizon is the negative log-probability of the correct match. Averaging across contexts and trained horizons gives the multi-horizon InfoNCE objective:

$$
-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}
\sum_{(i,t,k)\in\mathcal{I}}
\log
\frac{\exp(s_{ii}^{(k)})}
{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{B}\exp(s_{ij}^{(k)})},
$$

where $\mathcal{I}$ is the set of valid context, sequence, and horizon combinations in the batch. This is equation 3.5 aimed at a different task. Instead of identifying which view belongs to the same observation, it identifies which future latent belongs to the current context.

The model is trained at several horizons. A one-step prediction head scores the latent at (t+1); a two-step head scores (t+2); a three-step head scores (t+3). Each head has its own matrix $W_k$. The context is shared, so its representation must support identification of future latents at each of these horizons.

The model does not directly regress the future latent with a squared-error objective. It does not take a predicted vector, feed that vector back into the model, and recursively generate a long sequence. It learns compatibility scores that rank the correct future above other candidates. Those are distinct tasks, and the evaluation must respect that distinction.

#### The implementation

In the repository, worldmodels/models/temporal_cpc.py defines TemporalCPC. Its forward method encodes a batch of image sequences, runs the GRU, and calculates cross-entropy over candidate future latents at each valid context and horizon.

**Listing 3.12 — CPC context and compatibility scoring**

Source: `worldmodels/models/temporal_cpc.py`. The full runner lives in `ch03/11_temporal_cpc.py`.

```python
z_seq = self.encode_sequence(x_seq)
c_seq, _ = self.gru(z_seq)

b, t, _ = z_seq.shape
cpc_sum = torch.zeros((), device=x_seq.device)
n_cpc = 0

for t_idx in range(0, t - self.k_steps):
    c_t = c_seq[:, t_idx]
    for k in range(self.k_steps):
        target = z_seq[:, t_idx + 1 + k]
        pred = c_t @ self.w_k[k].T
        logits = pred @ target.T
        labels = torch.arange(b, device=x_seq.device)
        cpc_sum = cpc_sum + F.cross_entropy(logits, labels)
        n_cpc += 1

return cpc_sum / n_cpc
```

The matrix multiplication produces a batch-by-batch score matrix. Row $i$ contains the scores assigned by context $i$ to every candidate future latent in that batch. The correct target lies on the diagonal, because context and future target come from the same sequence. Cross-entropy encourages the diagonal score to dominate its row.

The GRU is unidirectional. Although the implementation processes a tensor containing multiple time steps at once, the context output at index $t$ is computed causally from the sequence up to that time step. Future latents are targets used to calculate the loss; they are not inputs to the context at $t$.

WARNING — Causality. A context used to predict the future may contain the current observation and earlier observations, but not the future target. If future frames influence the context, the model can identify the target using information that would not be available at prediction time.

#### What enters the experiment

The CPC experiment uses Cartpole Balance frames collected under a uniform random policy. The model receives sequences of images. It does not receive the actions that generated those sequences.

That omission matters. In a controlled physical system, the next state depends not only on the current state but also on the action applied and the system's dynamics. CPC here learns from a sequence generated by an unknown-to-the-model action stream. Its context must infer useful information from the visual history alone.

This experiment is therefore not a controlled comparison between action-conditioned and action-free prediction. It does not tell us how much actions would improve the results. The missing action input is a limitation of this setup and one reason the next chapter treats predictive representations and action-conditioning as separate design questions.

### 3.7.3 CPC evaluation: future retrieval and state probes

A low CPC loss means that the correct future latent receives high probability under the model's candidate distribution. But the loss alone is harder to interpret than a direct retrieval measurement. We therefore evaluate the trained compatibility scores on held-out sequence windows and ask whether the correct future latent ranks above the other candidates.

This evaluation is aligned with the objective CPC actually trains. It is not a physical-state rollout experiment.

#### Evaluation protocol

The committed run uses five trained model seeds: 0 through 4. Each model was trained for 20 epochs with a batch size of 32, learning rate $10^{-3}$, encoder latent dimension 16, GRU context dimension 32, and three prediction horizons. The training configuration caps the data at 4,000 training sequence windows and 1,000 validation sequence windows, each of length 16 frames.

For the primary retrieval evaluation, the experiment selects 200 non-overlapping, episode-safe windows from the validation split. The selection is balanced across five validation episodes, with 40 windows per episode. The windows do not overlap within their source episodes, and they are shuffled into evaluation batches so that candidate sequences are not adjacent windows presented in temporal order. These 200 windows are not 200 independent trajectories; they come from only five validation episodes.

Evaluation compares each context's correct future latent with the corresponding future latents from other sequences in the same batch. There are six full batches of 32 candidates and a final batch of eight. The chance level is consequently computed for this query-weighted, in-batch protocol:

$$
\frac{192(1/32)+8(1/8)}{200}=0.035=3.5\%
$$

The random top-1 chance rate is therefore 3.5%, rather than $1/200$. The test does not rank each context against all 200 windows simultaneously. That distinction is part of the result, not a detail to omit from the caption.

#### Future-latent retrieval

The top-1 retrieval rate is the fraction of queries for which the matching future latent receives the highest score in its batch. Top-5 retrieval counts a match as correct if it appears among the five highest-scoring candidates. The positive-minus-hardest-negative margin measures the score gap between the correct future and the most competitive incorrect candidate. Because the logits are raw bilinear scores, that margin is a score difference, not a probability.

Top-1 retrieval is high at all three horizons and declines from 95.0% at one step to 92.4% at three steps. InfoNCE loss rises with horizon, while the margin between the positive and hardest negative decreases. These metrics are consistent with identifying the matching future latent becoming less separated from other candidates at longer horizons, even though the correct candidate remains ranked first most of the time.

Top-5 retrieval reaches 100% in each seed at all three horizons. This tells us that the positive candidate reliably appears among the five highest-scoring candidates in the batches used for evaluation. It does not mean the model can predict five steps into the future with perfect physical accuracy.

The candidate protocol also limits the claim. Candidates come from only the selected validation windows and are compared within batches; the benchmark does not test retrieval across every possible future state or every possible trajectory. The five episodes contribute many windows, but windows from the same episode are not independent evidence of generalization to wholly new environments.

The recurrent context has a numerically higher current cart-position R² than the static encoder: 0.805 versus 0.770. Its mean next-state R² across the four state variables is also numerically higher, at 0.259 ± 0.016, compared with 0.238 ± 0.009 for the static representation. The per-variable measurements show that this change is limited. Cart velocity remains very weakly decodable, and pole angular velocity remains negative under both representations.

Negative R² means that the probe performs worse on the validation samples than a constant predictor based on the validation-target mean. It does not prove that the representation contains no information about that variable; it says the fitted linear readout does not recover it effectively under this evaluation.

The variation across seeds also matters. The context's cart-position score varies more from seed to seed than the static score, and the study does not establish statistical significance for the differences between the two representations. It would be too strong to conclude that adding a GRU solves state estimation or makes velocity available. The measurement supports a more limited result: the recurrent context changes what is linearly available, but the change is not uniform across physical variables.

#### What the experiment establishes

The primary result is that the trained CPC compatibility function identifies matching future latents substantially above the protocol's 3.5% random-candidate chance level. Its retrieval performance remains high over three trained horizons, while the loss and margin indicate somewhat less separation at longer horizons.

The state probes provide a complementary picture. Current position is relatively well represented, but velocity remains poorly decoded from both the static latent and the recurrent context. High future-latent retrieval therefore does not imply that every physical variable is explicitly available in the representation. Nor does it establish that the model can predict a physical state recursively, remain stable across long rollouts, or support a useful planner.

The experiment also receives no action input. Because the frames were generated under a random policy, the learned context does not distinguish state-driven change from the effects of the actions taken. The study is not a controlled test of action-conditioning; it simply does not include it. We must not interpret the retrieval score as action-conditioned transition prediction or as a universal ceiling on what CPC can achieve.

WARNING — A GRU is a placeholder, not a memory guarantee. The context vector summarizes a finite sequence through a learned recurrent state. A GRU does not guarantee that information needed over long periods will be retained. The memory chapter returns to that question using an environment designed to test long-term memory, rather than treating this short-window retrieval task as a memory benchmark.

CPC provides a bridge between the objectives in this chapter and the predictive representations discussed next. Instead of enforcing agreement between two observations or rebuilding missing pixels, it asks a context to identify a future embedding. Chapter 4 develops the joint-embedding predictive idea further through JEPA architectures, with a different emphasis on learned prediction targets and, later, action-conditioning.

We now have several ways to train a visual representation. Contrastive learning encourages agreement across views. Non-contrastive objectives add mechanisms to prevent collapse. Masked prediction trains a model to reconstruct hidden pixels, and CPC asks a context to identify a future representation.

These methods differ in what they reward, but the agent should not need a different interface for every training objective. Once an encoder has been trained, the agent needs a component that accepts an observation and returns a representation. If the agent maintains a recurrent state, the same component must also update that state as new observations arrive.

This is where the chapter's work returns to the agent loop from Chapter 1. We will use a trained contrastive encoder behind a common interface. The dynamics and reward components remain placeholders; this section assembles the representation component rather than claiming to complete the world model.
