# 3.3 Contrastive learning from first principles

Section 3.2 showed how to manufacture a training target by transforming an observation. But the target determines what the encoder is asked to learn, and recognizing a rotation does not necessarily require a useful physical state.

Contrastive learning changes the question. Instead of asking which transformation was applied, it asks which observations came from the same source. We take two altered views of one frame and train a model to identify them as a pair among other views.

The idea is simple. Making two views agree encourages the encoder to retain information shared by both. Making it distinguish them from other views encourages the encoder to retain information that identifies the source. The mathematics turns this idea into a classification loss.

### 3.3.1 Views, positives, and negatives

A view of an observation $x$ is an altered copy of it. Write the view as

$$
\tilde{x}=t(x),
$$

where $t$ is a randomly selected transformation. The transformation is applied to the image, not to the underlying physical system.

In this chapter, the view pipeline uses two kinds of alteration. It shifts the image by a random integer offset of up to three pixels in each direction, then applies random brightness and contrast changes. The brightness offset is sampled from $[-0.1,0.1]$, and the contrast factor from $[0.9,1.1]. The values are clipped to the valid image range.

These changes are deliberately modest. They alter pixel values and image alignment, but they are intended to leave the physical situation unchanged.

A positive pair consists of two independently transformed views of the same frame. A negative pair consists of views from different source frames. The distinction comes from how we created the data, so no human label is needed.

The training process operates on a batch of $N$ source frames. Each source frame produces two views, giving $2N$ images. The encoder maps these images to representations $z_1,\ldots,z_{2N}$.

For a particular view $i$, call it the anchor. Its positive, written $i^+$, is the other view of the same original frame. The remaining views are its negatives. Excluding the anchor itself, there are $2N-1$ candidates: one positive and $2N-2$ negatives.

Thus,

$$
K=2N-2,
$$

where $K$ is the number of negatives for each anchor. For example, a batch of $N=3$ frames produces six views. Each anchor has one positive and four negatives.

The transformation $t$ is a design decision, not a cosmetic detail. If the encoder must identify two views of the same frame, changes introduced randomly by $t$ cannot reliably identify the source. The encoder is therefore encouraged to become insensitive to those changes.

This is the intended benefit of the augmentation pipeline. If brightness changes do not alter the physical state, the representation should ideally remain useful despite them. But the same reasoning also creates a risk: if the transformation changes something important, the objective may reward the encoder for ignoring it.

A shift of a few pixels may be harmless for a cart-pole. A shift large enough to move the cart or pole out of view could erase information needed to estimate the state. The positive-pair definition therefore specifies which changes should be treated as irrelevant. The following equations show how the loss enforces this requirement.

The view pipeline is small, but its consequences run through the entire method. Contrastive learning can encourage invariance only to transformations that the training process actually applies. The choice of transformations is therefore part of the learning objective, even though it does not appear as a term in the loss equation.

### 3.3.2 From similarity to a loss

We now need a numerical measure of how well two representations match. We will build the objective in three steps: define a similarity score, turn the candidate views into a classification problem, and minimize the classification error.

#### Step 1: Measure similarity

We use cosine similarity. For two nonzero vectors $u$ and $v$, it is the cosine of the angle between them:

$$
\operatorname{sim}(u,v)=
\frac{u^\top v}{\lVert u\rVert_2\lVert v\rVert_2}.
\tag{3.3}
$$

Cosine similarity lies between $-1$ and $1$. A value near $1$ means the vectors point in similar directions; a value near $-1$ means they point in opposite directions. A value near zero means they are approximately orthogonal.

The normalization in the denominator means that the score depends on direction rather than vector length. Simply multiplying a representation by a positive constant cannot increase its cosine similarity with another vector.

#### Step 2: Turn similarity into a classification problem

Fix an anchor $i$. There are $2N-1$ candidate views, excluding the anchor itself. We want a classifier to assign the highest probability to the positive $i^+$.

For each candidate $j$, divide the similarity by a positive temperature $\tau$, then apply softmax across the candidates:

$$
p_i(j)=
\frac{
\exp\big(\operatorname{sim}(z_i,z_j)/\tau\big)
}{
\displaystyle\sum_{k\ne i}
\exp\big(\operatorname{sim}(z_i,z_k)/\tau\big)
},
\qquad j\ne i.
\tag{3.4}
$$

Here, $p_i(j)$ is the probability assigned to candidate $j$ being the other view of the anchor's source frame. The correct answer is $i^+$.

The temperature $\tau$ controls how sharply differences in similarity affect the probabilities. A smaller temperature magnifies differences between candidates. A larger temperature makes their probabilities more uniform.

#### Step 3: Penalize a wrong answer

We use the negative logarithm of the probability assigned to the positive. The loss for anchor $i$ is

$$
\ell_i=-\log p_i(i^+).
$$

A correct answer assigned high probability receives a small loss. A positive assigned low probability receives a large loss. Averaging across all $2N$ anchors gives the normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy loss, or NT-Xent:

$$
\frac{1}{2N}
\sum_{i=1}^{2N}-\log p_i(i^+)
\tag{3.5}
$$

This is the same basic cross-entropy as in equation 3.2, but the classification problem is different. Rotation prediction chooses among four transformations. NT-Xent chooses among the other views in the batch, with a different correct class for each anchor.

Consider a batch with $N=2$, so there are four views. Each anchor has three candidate views. Suppose an anchor has cosine similarities of $0.80$ with its positive and $0.10$ and $-0.30$ with the two negatives. Let the temperature be $\tau=0.5$.

Dividing the similarities by the temperature gives logits of $1.6$, $0.2$, and $-0.6$. Their exponentials are approximately $4.953$, $1.221$, and $0.549$. The sum is $6.723$, so the softmax assigns probabilities of approximately $0.737$, $0.182$, and $0.082$.

The positive has probability $0.737$, giving the anchor a loss of

$$
-\log(0.737)\approx0.306.
$$

If the classifier cannot distinguish the positive and assigns the same probability to all three candidates, it assigns probability $1/3$ to the positive and has loss

$$
-\log(1/3)=\log3\approx1.099.
$$

For general batch size $N$, an exactly uniform probability distribution over the candidates gives loss $\log(2N-1)$. This is the uninformative reference value for this classification problem.

> **WARNING — Comparing NT-Xent losses**

Compare NT-Xent loss values only when the batch size and temperature are the same. The number of candidate views changes with batch size, and the temperature changes the softmax probabilities. A lower numerical loss under a different configuration does not necessarily mean a better representation.

> **TIP — Batch size controls the negatives**

Batch size sets the number of negatives: $K=2N-2$. A larger batch asks the model to distinguish the positive from more candidates, but it also requires memory for more views and their similarity scores.

### 3.3.3 What InfoNCE actually optimizes

NT-Xent is one instance of the broader InfoNCE objective, used in contrastive predictive coding (van den Oord et al., 2018) and closely related to the contrastive formulation used by SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020). For each anchor, InfoNCE compares one positive with $K$ negatives. Its mathematical properties help explain both what contrastive learning encourages and what it cannot guarantee.

First, we need a measure of shared information.

> **DEFINITION — Mutual information**

The mutual information $I(a;b)$ between random variables $a$ and $b$ measures how much knowing one reduces uncertainty about the other. It is the expected log ratio between their joint distribution and the product of their marginal distributions:

$$
I(a;b)=
\mathbb{E}_{p(a,b)}
\left[
\log\frac{p(a,b)}{p(a)p(b)}
\right].
$$

Mutual information is zero when $a$ and $b$ are independent. With natural logarithms, it is measured in nats.

Let $a$ and $b$ be the representations of two views of the same frame. This is the positive pair. Let $b_1,\ldots,b_K$ be representations of negative views drawn independently from the marginal distribution of $b$. For a positive score function (f$a,b$), InfoNCE is

$$
-\mathbb{E}
\left[
\log
\frac{f(a,b)}
{f(a,b)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}f(a,b_k)}
\right].
$$

For NT-Xent, the score uses exponentiated cosine similarity divided by temperature. Under the standard sampling assumptions, InfoNCE satisfies the lower bound

$$
\boxed{
I(a;b)
\ge
\log(K+1)-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{InfoNCE}}
}
\tag{3.6}
$$

This is a result about the information shared by the two views' representations, not a direct guarantee that the representation captures the physical state.

**Proof note.** The mutual-information lower bound follows from the Bayes-optimal classifier over one positive and $K$ marginal negatives. Appendix A gives the complete change-of-measure argument; here we use the bound only as an interpretation of shared view information, not as a guarantee that physical state is retained.

Equation 3.6 has two useful implications. First, lowering the InfoNCE loss raises the conventional lower bound on the information shared by the positive pair. Second, the bound is capped by $\log(K+1)$, because the loss cannot be negative. A finite batch therefore limits how much shared information this objective can certify.

The assumptions matter. In the chapter's implementation, the negatives come from other frames in a batch, and two views from the same source frame are themselves related. Frames drawn from trajectories may also be correlated. The textbook mutual-information bound is therefore a useful interpretation of the objective, not an exact measurement of the trained model's mutual information.

The deeper limitation follows from how the views are constructed. Each view is a randomly transformed copy of the same source frame $x$. If $z_1$ and $z_2$ are their representations, the data-processing inequality gives

$$
I(z_1;z_2)
\le
I(\tilde{x}_1;\tilde{x}_2)
\le
I(x;\tilde{x}_1).
$$

The information shared by both views is constrained by what survives the transformations. A detail that changes independently between views cannot reliably identify a positive pair. Conversely, information preserved across both views can help.

But shared information is not the same as the information needed by a world model. The objective asks the encoder to distinguish one frame's views from other frames' views. It does not explicitly ask it to preserve every state variable useful for prediction or planning.

The gradient in equation 3.7 separates attraction toward the positive from repulsion by negatives. The coefficient of each negative is its current softmax probability, so hard negatives receive more weight. Appendix A derives the gradient and the cosine-similarity derivative step by step.

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{z_i}\ell_i
={}&
-\frac{1-p_i(i^+)}{\tau}
\nabla_{z_i}\operatorname{sim}(z_i,z_{i^+})\\
&+
\sum_{k\notin{i,i^+}}
\frac{p_i(k)}{\tau}
\nabla_{z_i}\operatorname{sim}(z_i,z_k).
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{3.7}
$$

The first term attracts the anchor toward its positive. The second term pushes it away from the negatives, weighted by their current probabilities.

Four observations follow.

Balanced forces: the sum of the negative probabilities is $1-p_i(i^+)$, so the total negative weight equals the attraction weight for this anchor.

Self-limiting updates: when the positive already receives probability close to one, both attraction and repulsion become small.

Hard negatives matter most: a negative with high similarity receives a larger probability and a stronger push.

Temperature changes the emphasis: lowering $\tau$ makes the probability distribution more concentrated, increasing the relative influence of the most competitive negative.

In the numerical example above, the attraction weight is $(1-0.737)/0.5\approx0.527$. The two negative weights are $0.182/0.5=0.364$ and $0.082/0.5=0.164$. Their sum is approximately equal to the attraction weight, with the small discrepancy due to rounding. Gradient descent applies the update in the opposite direction of the gradient, increasing similarity to the positive and decreasing similarity to negatives.

equation 3.7 describes the anchor's role in one loss. The same representation also serves as a candidate in the losses of other anchors; those additional terms contribute to its complete gradient.

The key limitation remains: these gradients arrange the representations to solve the specified identification problem. The objective does not know whether the features used for identification are cart position, pole angle, texture, or some combination. That is why the following experiments evaluate the encoder rather than treating a falling loss as proof of a useful state.

### 3.3.4 Training a contrastive encoder

We now train the loss on rendered frames from cartpole_balance. The model has two parts: a convolutional encoder that produces the representation used later, and a projection head used during contrastive training.

The encoder in worldmodels/models/encoders.py contains four convolutional layers with $4\times4$ kernels and stride two. Starting with 64 × 64 RGB input, the spatial resolution is reduced as follows:

$$
64\rightarrow32\rightarrow16\rightarrow8\rightarrow4.
$$

The channel count grows from 32 to 64, 128, and 256. The final 256 × 4 × 4 feature map is flattened and projected to a 16-dimensional vector $z=f_\theta(x)$.

**Listing 3.3 — The convolutional encoder and projection head**

Source: worldmodels/models/encoders.py. This excerpt is condensed from the canonical implementation, with the architecture and behavior unchanged.

```python
class ConvEncoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(
        self,
        in_channels: int = 3,
        latent_dim: int = 16,
        base_channels: int = 32,
    ) -> None:
        super().__init__()
        self.in_channels = in_channels
        self.latent_dim = latent_dim
        self.conv_net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv2d(in_channels, base_channels, 4, 2, 1),
            nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
            nn.Conv2d(base_channels, base_channels * 2, 4, 2, 1),
            nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
            nn.Conv2d(base_channels * 2, base_channels * 4, 4, 2, 1),
            nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
            nn.Conv2d(base_channels * 4, base_channels * 8, 4, 2, 1),
            nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
        )
        flat_dim = (base_channels * 8) * 4 * 4
        self.fc = nn.Linear(flat_dim, latent_dim)

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        if x.ndim != 4:
            raise ValueError("ConvEncoder expects input shaped (B, C, H, W)")
        features = self.conv_net(x)
        return self.fc(torch.flatten(features, start_dim=1))

class ProjectionHead(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_dim: int = 16, hidden_dim: int = 64, out_dim: int = 16) -> None:
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(in_dim, hidden_dim),
            nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
            nn.Linear(hidden_dim, out_dim),
        )

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        return self.net(x)
```

A Each convolution halves the spatial height and width.

B Maps the flattened feature map to the 16-dimensional representation.

C Maps the representation to the projection used by the contrastive loss.

With the default channel count, the encoder has 755,696 parameters and the projection head has 2,128. The encoder uses no batch normalization, so the representation of one frame does not depend on the other images in its batch.

The loss is applied to the projection $p=g_\phi(z)$, not directly to $z$. This follows the design used in SimCLR, where a nonlinear projection head is used during contrastive pretraining while the representation before that head is retained for downstream tasks (Chen et al., 2020). The reason for separating the two is practical: the projection can become invariant to some details needed by later tasks, while the encoder representation remains available to downstream components. This chapter adopts that setup without claiming that the two alternatives have been compared experimentally here.

**Listing 3.4 — The NT-Xent loss**

Source: worldmodels/losses/ntxent.py, the canonical implementation.

```python
def nt_xent_loss(z1, z2, temperature=0.5):
    n = z1.shape[0]
    device = z1.device
    z1 = F.normalize(z1, dim=1)                         #A
    z2 = F.normalize(z2, dim=1)
    reps = torch.cat([z1, z2], dim=0)                  #B
    logits = torch.matmul(reps, reps.T) / temperature #C
    eye = torch.eye(2 * n, dtype=torch.bool, device=device)
    logits = logits.masked_fill(eye, -1e9)             #D
    labels = torch.cat([
        torch.arange(n, 2 * n, device=device),          #E
        torch.arange(0, n, device=device),
    ])
    return F.cross_entropy(logits, labels)              #F
```

A Normalizes each representation so its dot product becomes cosine similarity.

B Concatenates the two views into one batch of $2N$ vectors.

C Computes all pairwise similarities, scaled by the temperature.

D Removes self-similarity from each anchor's candidate set.

E Identifies the positive row for each view.

F Applies cross-entropy to the positive labels, implementing equation 3.5.

Masking the diagonal excludes the anchor itself from the softmax denominator. The label indices identify the other view of the same frame: the positive of row $i$ is row $i+N$, and the positive of row $i+N$ is row $i$.

The repository also includes ch03/03_ntxent_from_scratch.py. It compares a direct loop-based implementation of the mathematical definition with the vectorized function above, checking both loss values and gradients. It is a verification script, rather than a second implementation intended for the main training run.

### 3.3.5 Reading the state back with a linear probe

The simulator provides the physical state associated with each rendered frame. That state never enters the self-supervised loss. We can use it after training to ask which physical quantities are available in the representation.

A linear probe is a simple evaluation model fitted to a frozen encoder. We compute the representation $z$, freeze the encoder, and fit a linear map from $z$ to the evaluation targets. We then evaluate that map on held-out observations. This measures the information that a linear readout can recover; it does not test whether an arbitrary nonlinear decoder could recover more.

For cart-pole balance, the raw state contains four variables: cart position, pole angle, cart velocity, and pole angular velocity. The pole angle needs special treatment. The simulator stores its hinge angle as an unwrapped value, so angles separated by a full revolution can have different numbers but represent the same physical orientation. An angle of $0.1$ radians and one of $0.1+2\pi$ radians produce the same orientation.

To make a linear probe appropriate for this variable, the evaluation targets replace the angle $\theta$ with $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$. The transformed target therefore has five components: cart position, $\sin\theta$, $\cos\theta$, cart velocity, and pole angular velocity. The probe reconstructs an angle using the inverse tangent, and the repository scores angle predictions with a circular $R^2$-like metric. This avoids treating a prediction across the angular wrap boundary as a large error simply because the numerical angle differs by nearly $2\pi$.

Let $\check z_n$ be a representation standardized using the training-set feature means and standard deviations, and let $\tilde y_n$ be the transformed target vector. The probe fits a ridge-regression readout:

$$
\boxed{
\min_{W,b}
\sum_{n=1}^{M}
\left\lVert
\tilde y_n-W\check z_n-b
\right\rVert_2^2
+
\alpha\lVert W\rVert_F^2
}
\tag{3.8}
$$

Here, $M$ is the number of training examples used to fit the probe; $W$ and $b$ are the linear weights and bias; and $\alpha$ controls the ridge penalty. The current probe implementation uses $\alpha=10^{-3}$, standardizes the frozen features, and solves the ridge problem by least squares. The encoder is not updated when the probe is fitted.

**Listing 3.6 — Fitting a ridge probe to frozen features**

Source: worldmodels/eval/probes.py, the canonical LinearProbe implementation.

```python
def fit(self, x, y):
    x_scaled = self._fit_feature_scaler(x)             #A
    n, d = x_scaled.shape
    x_bias = np.concatenate(
        [x_scaled, np.ones((n, 1))], axis=1
    )                                                  #B
    reg = np.zeros((d + 1, d + 1))
    reg[:d, :d] = np.sqrt(self.alpha) * np.eye(d)      #C
    augmented_x = np.vstack([x_bias, reg])
    augmented_y = np.vstack([
        y, np.zeros((d + 1, y.shape[1]))
    ])
    self.weights, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(
        augmented_x, augmented_y, rcond=None
    )                                                  #D
    return self
```

A Standardizes features with statistics from the probe's training data.

B Adds a bias column, which is not penalized.

C Adds the rows that implement the ridge penalty.

D Solves the augmented least-squares problem corresponding to equation 3.8.

The score is computed on held-out validation frames. For ordinary state variables, $R^2=1$ is perfect prediction, $R^2=0$ matches the constant-mean baseline, and a negative value is worse than that baseline. Angular targets use the circular score described above.

A probe's limitations are important. A high score means the physical variable is available to a linear readout. A low or negative score does not prove the information is absent from $z$; it may be represented nonlinearly, or the probe may be limited by the data and fitting setup. The measurement tells us what is explicitly and linearly available under this evaluation, not everything the encoder may know.

The result is more limited than the hypothesis that a second frame will recover velocity. Position is more readily decoded than velocity from a single frame, but stacking two frames does not reliably improve the velocity measurements in this configuration. Cart-velocity $R^2$ increases by only about $0.002$, while the pole-angular-velocity score becomes slightly worse. The position scores also do not improve.

This is a result about the tested image resolution, view transformations, encoder, data sampling, and training budget. It does not prove that velocity cannot be inferred from image sequences. It shows that simply providing two consecutive frames was not enough to make the tested velocities reliably available to this linear probe.

The main contrastive training run and this aliasing diagnostic also use different training budgets, so their results should not be treated as a controlled comparison with all other factors held fixed. In both cases, the probes use state labels only for evaluation. The contrastive objective itself still sees rendered frames and generated view pairs.

The larger lesson is the one that motivated this chapter. The encoder is optimized for the task stated by its loss. NT-Xent rewards representations that distinguish a frame's augmented views from views of other frames. That can organize representations and make source identity more accessible, but the loss does not explicitly require the physical state needed by a world model. To learn that state, the objective and the information available to the encoder matter.

The next section examines a failure that can occur even before asking which physical variables are represented: collapse, in which different inputs map to nearly identical representations and the model satisfies a similarity objective without learning a useful distinction.

3.4 The collapse problem and how it is avoided

Section 3.3 introduced a loss that brings positive views together and separates them from negative views. The negative pairs matter: they make it difficult for the encoder to map every input to the same representation. But contrastive learning is not the only way to train an encoder from paired views. Other methods remove the negatives and use a different mechanism to prevent collapse.

A representation collapses when different inputs receive the same, or nearly the same, representation. This section demonstrates why simple attraction between positive views is insufficient, then examines three mechanisms that avoid collapse without negative pairs: stop-gradient, a slowly moving target network, and redundancy reduction. Finally, we study VICReg, which makes the requirements on the representation explicit in its loss.
