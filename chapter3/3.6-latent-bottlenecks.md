# 3.6 Latent bottlenecks and compact state

### 3.6.1 The information bottleneck as a lens

Let $X$ denote an observation, $Z$ its learned representation, and $Y$ a task-relevant variable. For a world model, $X$ might be an image and $Y$ might describe the physical state needed to predict what happens next.

The information bottleneck asks for a representation that preserves information relevant to $Y$ while discarding as much unnecessary information about $X$ as possible. One way to express this trade-off is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB}}
= I(Z;X)-\beta I(Z;Y).
\tag{3.19}
$$

Here, $I(A;B)$ is the mutual information between random variables $A$ and $B$. It measures statistical dependence: it is zero when the variables are independent and increases as knowing one variable provides more information about the other. The coefficient $\beta$ controls the exchange between compression and relevance.

The two terms pull in opposite directions.

**Compression.** Minimizing $I(Z;X)$ discourages the representation from carrying information about every detail of the input.

**Relevance.** Minimizing the full loss rewards a large $I(Z;Y)$, because that term is subtracted. The representation should preserve information that helps distinguish task-relevant outcomes.

When $\beta$ is small, compression receives more weight relative to relevance. As $\beta$ increases, preserving information about $Y$ becomes more important. The best trade-off depends on which information the task actually needs.

This gives a useful interpretation of a compact state. If an image contains a cart, a pole, a floor, lighting, and background details, the agent may not need to encode all those details equally. It needs enough information to distinguish states that matter for prediction and control. But compression must stop before two physically different situations that require different decisions become indistinguishable in the representation.

That is the connection to the contracts from Chapter 2. A representation needs sufficiency for the task, but minimality asks it not to retain unnecessary detail. A bottleneck can help with the second goal while making the first harder to satisfy.

There is an important distinction between this information-theoretic objective and simply choosing a small vector. A deterministic encoder with a low-dimensional continuous output is not, by that fact alone, optimizing $I(Z;X)$. In formal information-bottleneck formulations, the relationship between input and representation is treated probabilistically, and the mutual-information terms must be defined and optimized or bounded appropriately. A deterministic autoencoder with a narrow latent layer imposes a practical capacity constraint; it does not automatically implement equation 3.19.

The same distinction applies to VICReg. Its invariance, variance, and covariance penalties encourage particular properties of learned embeddings, but VICReg does not optimize the information-bottleneck objective in equation 3.19. The equation is a conceptual lens for understanding what we want from a representation, not a claim that every self-supervised objective is secretly an information-bottleneck algorithm.

In this chapter's experiments, the simulator's physics state remains an evaluation target. The contrastive loss does not receive that state as a training label, and the latent-dimension sweep does not calculate mutual information directly. We use a linear probe to measure one concrete property: how much of the state is linearly recoverable from the learned features.

### 3.6.2 Choosing the latent dimension

The practical question is how many dimensions the encoder needs. It is tempting to set the latent dimension equal to the number of physical state variables. That is not a reliable rule.

A physical variable need not map to one latent coordinate. A learned feature can combine several state variables, and a single variable may require several features to become easy to decode. This is especially relevant to angles: a linear probe can have difficulty reading an angle directly near the wraparound boundary, so the evaluation uses periodic encoding and a circular R² score for angular positions.

Nor does a larger latent dimension automatically make the representation better. The encoder may fail to learn useful features within the available training budget, or its additional coordinates may not improve the information that the probe can recover. The dimension is a capacity choice; the probe is a measurement of the result.

#### The hypothesis

The benchmark supplies two tasks with different physical-state dimensions:

Cartpole Balance: four state variables.

Cheetah Run: eighteen state variables.

The hypothesis is that Cartpole will reach a useful probe-performance plateau at a smaller latent dimension, while Cheetah will benefit from larger representations for longer. At $d=2$, Cartpole's representation may be too restrictive to preserve the variation needed to distinguish its physical states.

This hypothesis concerns the performance of the complete learned representation, not just the arithmetic difference between the number of latent dimensions and the number of state variables. A four-variable system does not imply that a four-dimensional latent is sufficient, nor that four is the optimal dimension for a linear probe.

#### The experiment

The repository's ch03/10_bottleneck_sweep.py trains a separate contrastive encoder for every combination of task, latent dimension, and random seed. The dimensions are $d\in{2,4,8,16,64,256}$, with seeds 0, 1, and 2. Each run uses the convolutional encoder from Section 3.3, with a projection head whose output dimension matches $d$.

The run uses Adam with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, a batch size of 64, and 10 training epochs. It selects 3,000 training frames and 1,000 validation frames, spreading the selected indices across episodes rather than taking a single prefix of each dataset. The contrastive objective operates on two augmented views of each frame. Once training is complete, the linear probe is fitted to the frozen encoder features, not the projection head's output.

**Listing 3.11 — The latent-dimension sweep driver**

Source: `ch03/10_bottleneck_sweep.py`. The full runner handles task configuration, seeds, episode-aware sampling, checkpointing, metrics, and plot generation.

```python
def train_and_evaluate(
    train_loader, val_loader, latent_dim, seed, args, device
):
    set_seed(seed)
    encoder = ConvEncoder(latent_dim=latent_dim).to(device)
    projector = ProjectionHead(latent_dim, 64, latent_dim).to(device)
    optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(
        list(encoder.parameters()) + list(projector.parameters()),
        lr=args.lr,
    )
    pipeline = ViewPipeline()

    for _ in range(args.epochs):
        encoder.train()
        projector.train()
        for batch in train_loader:
            x = batch["image"].to(device)
            v1, v2 = pipeline(x)
            loss = nt_xent_loss(
                projector(encoder(v1)),
                projector(encoder(v2)),
            )
            optimizer.zero_grad()
            loss.backward()
            optimizer.step()

    return evaluate_linear_probe(
        encoder, train_loader, val_loader, device
    )
```

The sweep measures held-out linear-probe R², averaged across the task's physical-state variables. The probe standardizes frozen latent features before fitting a ridge-regression readout. Its angular-state evaluation uses periodic handling, so the score is not distorted merely because an angle crosses a wraparound boundary. The reported spread is the standard deviation across the three trained seeds, not across independently collected environments.

#### The measured result

The generated Figure 3.14 records the result for both tasks.

For Cartpole Balance, the mean probe R² begins at −0.008 for $d=2$, rises to 0.119 at $d=4$, and reaches 0.231 at $d=16$. The strongest result in this sweep is 0.321 ± 0.005 at $d=64$. Increasing the dimension to $d=256$ does not improve it: the result is 0.317 ± 0.006. Under this configuration, performance has largely levelled off between 64 and 256 dimensions.

The result does not place the plateau at the task's four state variables. A four-dimensional output provides four real-valued coordinates, but that alone does not mean the encoder has organized those coordinates into a representation from which the physical state is easy to decode. At $d=4$, the probe R² of 0.119 remains weak. The poor score at $d=2$ is consistent with a restrictive representation losing useful state information, but this experiment does not explicitly identify pairs of physical states that have collided in latent space. The score alone is evidence of weak linear decodability, not a direct demonstration of a particular aliasing pair.

Cheetah Run behaves differently. Its mean probe R² rises from 0.060 ± 0.013 at $d=2$, to 0.238 ± 0.017 at $d=16$, and 0.301 ± 0.061 at $d=64$. At $d=256$, it reaches 0.422 ± 0.021, the best score measured for this task. Unlike Cartpole, Cheetah has not reached a clear plateau within the tested range.

These results support the direction of the hypothesis: the four-variable task approaches a plateau at a smaller dimension, while the eighteen-variable task continues to benefit from increased capacity through $d=256$. They do not establish the location of Cheetah's eventual plateau. The sweep tests six dimensions, and its highest tested value is not necessarily sufficient.

The experiment is also not a controlled comparison of latent dimensions in which all else is mathematically identical. Each point comes from a newly trained encoder and projection head. The result therefore reflects how the fixed architecture, contrastive objective, data, and training budget work at each output dimension. The evidence answers a practical question about this training setup, not a universal law connecting physical-state dimension to optimal latent size.

TIP — Choose the smallest dimension that has saturated. Start with a sweep rather than selecting $d$ by intuition. Look for the smallest dimension at which the measured state probe stops improving meaningfully, and include variation across seeds. If performance is still rising at the largest tested dimension, the sweep has not yet identified a plateau.

SIDEBAR — The bottleneck in one paragraph. The latent dimension limits the coordinates available to the representation. Too little capacity can make task-relevant distinctions difficult to preserve; more capacity can improve decodability, but also costs computation and may not help the task. The right dimension is not automatically the state-vector length. It is the smallest practical representation that retains enough information for the work the agent must do, judged by measurements relevant to that work.

The distinction between reconstruction, invariance, and predictive information now becomes more concrete. Section 3.5 showed that reconstructing pixels does not guarantee that state is linearly available. This section shows that even a representation with more dimensions is useful only to the extent that it preserves the distinctions a probe can recover. In the next section, the question changes from which static observations should agree to which future observation follows from the past.

3.7 Predicting over time: from invariance to prediction

The objectives in this chapter have asked an encoder to preserve particular properties of observations. Contrastive learning makes different views of one observation agree. Masked prediction asks a model to reconstruct missing pixels. Neither objective directly requires a representation of what will happen next.

For a world model, that is an important omission. An agent needs to distinguish observations not only by how they look, but also by how the situation may evolve. Velocity is a simple example. Two frames can look almost identical while corresponding to different velocities, and those velocities can lead to different futures.

This creates a tension with invariance. If we train a model to treat two observations at different times as interchangeable, we may encourage it to discard information about the change between them. To predict the future, we need a different question:

#### Given the past, which future is this?

Contrastive predictive coding $CPC$ turns that question into a classification problem over candidate future representations. It does not yet give us an action-conditioned world model. It gives us a way to learn and evaluate representations by asking whether the context of a sequence identifies its actual future.
